## 1. Equal status, and the presumption on the first schedule motion

**Attack (retroactivity).** "This reopens final custody decrees."

**Answer.**
- **Ohio's test.** Article II, § 28 bars laws that are *substantively* retroactive, meaning they
  impair vested rights or attach new burdens to past transactions (*Van Fossen v. Babcock & Wilcox*,
  36 Ohio St.3d 100 (1988); *Bielat v. Bielat*, 87 Ohio St.3d 350 (2000)) [C].
- **No vested right.** A custody decree is never a vested right. It is always modifiable on the
  child's best interest under continuing jurisdiction (R.C. 3109.04(E); *Braatz*).
- **Prospective operation.** Both mechanisms operate only on a future motion, and only from the date
  of the new order.
- **No new right to file.** *Braatz* already permits the schedule motion. The bill changes its
  starting point, which is a burden allocation in a future proceeding.

**Attack (due process).** "The sole custodian loses sole decision-making without a best-interest
finding."

**Answer.**
- There is notice and a hearing on the statutory grounds.
- What is lost is *sole* authority. It is shared with another fit legal parent who holds the same
  fundamental interest; *Troxel* gives no ranking between two fit parents.
- On deadlock, the status quo holds until a court decides (3109.044(F)).

**Attack (floodgate).** "Every old case comes back."

**Answer.**
- One presumption-backed motion per family.
- Restoration excludes violent parents.
- The presumption is rebuttable on a preponderance, using real stability evidence.
- The filing right already exists under *Braatz*.

The residual risk is political, not legal.

**Risk: low-moderate.**

## 2. Status vs. schedule

The text says it three times:
- 3109.042(B): status is not a schedule;
- 3109.04(E)(1)(c): status "does not entitle a parent to have the child at any time not provided for
  in the existing schedule";
- 3109.045(A): the order stands as written.

Self-help is reached by contempt, by the agency, and by 2919.23(E)(1).

**Risk: low.**

## 3. Unmarried parents before any order

**Attack.** "A father who becomes a custodian at the moment of acknowledgment can take an infant."

**Answer.**
- This is the position of every married parent before divorce.
- The remedies are the same:
  - an ex parte temporary order (3109.043; Civ.R. 75(N));
  - a same-day ex parte protection order (3113.31);
  - emergency custody (2151.31).
- Replacing a sex- and marital-status-based rule *reduces* equal-protection exposure.
- A no-removal rule for all parents is ready if needed (Document 10, Decision 4).

**Risk: low-moderate (political).**

## 4. The presumption and the floor

**Answer.**
- Five enacted states use the same presumption device (Kentucky, Arkansas, West Virginia, Florida,
  Missouri).
- It is rebuttable on the lowest civil standard, with exceptions for agreement, protection orders,
  and offenders.
- The floor matches Minnesota's enacted 25 percent, with four exceptions and least-restrictive means.
- The presumptive approval of agreed plans *increases* deference to parents' joint judgment, which is
  the core *Troxel* value.

**Risk: low.**

## 5. Court orders control; GALs and parenting coordinators

**Attack.** "GALs and parenting coordinators are governed by Supreme Court rules (Sup.R. 8.00–8.07 and
6.40–6.46). The General Assembly cannot regulate court personnel. The cross-examination right is
procedural, and procedure belongs to the Court (art. IV, § 5(B))."

**Answer.**
- **Who may change parental rights is substantive law.** The Rules of Superintendence are
  court-administration rules, not § 5(B) practice-and-procedure rules.
- **The bill conflicts with no rule. It codifies the rules' own limits:**
  - 8.06(A)(3): reports are "not … determinative";
  - 6.42(B): no "[s]ubstantive changes in parenting time" through coordination.
- **Cross-examination is a due-process right** that the Supreme Court itself recognized (*In re
  Hoffman*, 2002-Ohio-5368). The text says "call … as a witness and cross-examine," which operates
  within Evid.R. 611 and Sup.R. 8.06(A)(4) ("available to testify") rather than displacing them.
- **Independent review of coordinators** is what *Gregory* (2019-Ohio-5210) held due process
  requires.

**Risk: low.**

## 6. The parenting time enforcement agency

**Attack 1 (judicial power, art. IV, § 1).** "An executive county agency is deciding whether a court
order was violated and ordering a remedy. That is adjudication."

**Answer.**
- The agency's determination has **no force until a court enters it** (3109.047(F)).
- Any parent can stop entry by objecting within 14 days, which brings a hearing within 28 days
  decided anew.
- If no one objects, the court enters it unless it is contrary to law. That is the same structure the
  General Assembly already uses:
  - support review (R.C. 3119.63–3119.65: "the court shall issue a revised court child support order"
    absent a hearing request);
  - administrative paternity orders (R.C. 3111.49: final unless a court action is brought within 14
    days).
- Both have operated for decades. Minnesota's expeditor statute uses the same logic ("binding …
  unless vacated or modified by the court").

**Attack 2 (contempt power).** "Only courts punish disobedience."

**Answer.** Correct, and the agency does not:
- It may not find contempt or impose a penalty (3109.047(I)).
- It may only *initiate* contempt for the parent (2705.031(B)(2)), as CSEAs already do for support
  under (B)(1).

This respects *Hale v. State* (1896) and *Zakany v. Zakany* (1984).

**Attack 3 (due process for the responding parent).** "Default by silence deprives a parent of
parenting time without a hearing."

**Answer.**
- The responding parent loses no time. A make-up order *adds* time to the other parent.
- Notice is by mail and electronic means.
- There are two windows to respond: 14 days to the agency and 14 days to object.
- The objection gets a judicial hearing decided anew.
- Michigan treats silence as agreement (MCL 552.642); Ohio support review does the same.

**Attack 4 (the agency decides safety).** "Caseworkers will weigh abuse claims."

**Answer.** They cannot. A good-faith safety assertion sends the matter straight to the court
(3109.047(E)(2)).

**Attack 5 (evidence).** "Agency findings will be used as proof."

**Answer.**
- The bill does not make findings admissible. Ohio's Evid.R. 803(8) excludes investigative findings,
  and § 5(B) leaves admissibility to the Court.
- The record of activities is admissible under 803(8)(a).
- An entered determination is a court order, not evidence.

**Attack 6 (federal funding).** "Using CSEAs for parenting time jeopardizes Title IV-D funds."

**Answer.**
- The activities are declared non-IV-D and must be separately funded and accounted for (3109.047(I);
  Section 5(B)).
- Access and Visitation grants (42 U.S.C. 669b) may fund "visitation enforcement."
- The one-year delayed start (Section 3(E)) allows cost allocation.

**Attack 7 (independence).** "A CSEA may be a government entity closely tied to the county
prosecutor, which would then receive its own referral." R.C. 3125.10 permits any designated private or
government entity. Which counties, if any, use a prosecutor-housed CSEA was not verified.

**Answer.** The prosecutor's discretion is untouched, so the referral changes nothing about charging.
The notice duty still runs.

**Risk: low-moderate.** The model is tested in Ohio. The exposure is cost, not constitutionality.

## 7. The 36-month allegation limit

**Child safety:**
- The limit punishes only the *absence of any court decision*.
- The court has a 30-month self-set hearing and a 90-day hearing on request.
- A finding of truth continues the restriction indefinitely.
- New conduct supports a new restriction.
- Protection orders and Chapter 2151 cases are excluded.
- Emergency action is always preserved (3109.045(E)).

**Due process (the accuser):** a 45-day continuance is available for genuine need. **Due process
(the accused):** their own delay is not counted, so a parent cannot game the clock by stalling.

**Repackaging:** all allegations about pre-restriction conduct share one period, "however the
allegation is later described."

**Separation of powers:** case-processing deadlines with consequences are familiar Ohio law. R.C.
2151.35(B)(1) mandates *dismissal* of an abuse complaint after 90 + 45 days. This statute is milder:
the restriction expires; the case is not dismissed.

**"Resolved":** acquittals and agency dispositions are excluded.
- An acquittal means only "not proven beyond a reasonable doubt."
- A "substantiated" disposition may rest on "other forms of confirmation deemed valid by the PCSA"
  (Ohio Adm.Code 5180:2-1-01).

Both remain evidence.

**Risk: low-moderate.**

## 8. Knowingly false allegations

- All three elements are required: material falsity, knowledge, and purpose.
- Unsubstantiated, unprosecuted, dismissed, unproven, or mistaken reports are expressly not false.
- 2151.421 immunity is preserved.
- The consequences are restorative (make-up time and fees) plus a change in circumstances. There is
  no new crime; R.C. 2921.14 already exists.

**Risk: low-moderate.** Option: clear and convincing (Document 10, Decision 5).

## 9. Criminal clarifications, 2919.23(E)

- **Vagueness improves.** The bill *adds* a definite rule (court-ordered time plus no listed
  excuse), a defense for unclear orders, and a mens rea gloss.
- **Nothing is narrowed.** (A)(1) and its penalties are unchanged.
- **Ex post facto: none.** The bill applies to conduct after the effective date (Section 3(D)), and
  *Parra*/*Sprinkle* already treat the conduct as criminal.
- **The lawful-excuse rule.** Defining lawful excuse as negating "without privilege" puts the burden
  on the state to prove the absence of an excuse, as part of the element. That is more protective of
  defendants than an affirmative defense under R.C. 2901.05.

**Risk: low.**

## 10. Prosecutor notice

- It is a ministerial notice duty only.
- No reasons are required, the extension is self-executing, and a declination is unreviewable.
- *Master* (1996) and *Rankin* (2025-Ohio-4483) are respected.
- Mandamus may lie to compel the *notice*, never a charge.

**Risk: low.**

## 11. Genuine abuse victims (whole bill)

The safeguards:
1. No presumption against protection-order respondents or 3109.04(C) offenders.
2. Restrictions on a preponderance; no conviction needed.
3. Safety claims go to judges, never to agency staff.
4. Good-faith reports protected; "unsubstantiated" is not "false."
5. Protective action excused if reported, and not counted as failing to facilitate.
6. Protection orders, Chapter 2151, 2151.31, and 2151.421 preserved.
7. No "alienation" concept anywhere.
8. In criminal cases, the state bears the burden on lawful excuse.

**Remaining concerns:** the pre-order no-removal gap (Decision 4) and the 36-month limit, which is
mitigated by the 30-month hearing.

**Risk: low.**
