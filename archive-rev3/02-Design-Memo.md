# DOCUMENT 2 — DESIGN MEMO (REVISION 3)

**4 October 2026.** Revision 3 is a targeted correction of Revision 2, not a rewrite. The
architecture is unchanged:
- draft against current Ohio law;
- amend existing sections;
- add machinery only where Ohio has none.

Revision 2 is archived in `archive-rev2/`.

**Labels used below:**
- **[LAW]** existing Ohio law, verified;
- **[MODEL]** another state's enacted law;
- **[REC]** this memo's recommendation.

## Part 2. Issue 1 — The parenting enforcement agency

### What Ohio law allows

- **Contempt belongs to the courts.** The power to punish disobedience of a court order by contempt
  is inherent in the courts. The General Assembly may regulate it but may not abridge it (*Hale v.
  State*, 55 Ohio St. 210 (1896); *Zakany v. Zakany*, 9 Ohio St.3d 192 (1984)) [LAW].
  - **So the agency cannot find contempt, jail anyone, or impose a penalty for disobedience.**
- **Judicial power is vested in the courts** (Ohio Const. art. IV, § 1) [LAW]. An executive agency's
  determination may become binding only through a court, or after an opportunity for judicial
  review.
- **Ohio already uses this model in family law** [LAW]:
  - **Support review, R.C. 3119.63–3119.65.** The CSEA calculates a revised amount and notifies both
    parties that it will be submitted to the court "unless the obligor or obligee requests an
    administrative hearing … within fourteen days." If no one requests a hearing, "the court shall
    issue a revised court child support order."
  - **Administrative paternity, R.C. 3111.49.** An agency order is "final and enforceable" unless a
    party brings a court action within fourteen days.
  - **CSEAs already have a statutory link to parenting time.** R.C. 3125.03 directs the program to
    locate absent parents "for purposes of establishing and enforcing parenting time orders."
- **Evidence.** Ohio's Evid.R. 803(8), unlike the federal rule, admits public records of an
  agency's "activities" and "matters observed pursuant to duty," but **not** investigative findings
  [LAW]. The statute therefore does not try to make agency findings admissible. Uncontested
  determinations become court orders. Contested ones are decided anew by the court, with the agency's
  record of activities in the file.
- **Federal money.** Title IV-D matching funds are not available for parenting-time work. The federal
  Access and Visitation grant (42 U.S.C. 669b) may fund "visitation enforcement." So the bill
  requires separate accounting and names those funding sources [LAW/federal].

### The models

| Model | What a non-judge decides | How it becomes binding |
|---|---|---|
| Michigan Friend of the Court [MODEL] | Applies make-up time; starts contempt (MCL 552.641–.644) | A **court** office: "employee of the circuit court … under the direction and supervision of the chief judge" (MCL 552.503). An unanswered make-up notice is deemed agreement (MCL 552.642) |
| Minnesota parenting time expeditor [MODEL] | May "make a determination as to whether the existing parenting time order has been violated" (Minn. Stat. 518.1751) | "[B]inding … unless vacated or modified by the court." Domestic abuse claims are exempt |
| Ohio support review [LAW] | Revised support amount | The court enters it if no objection within 14 days |

### The choice

**Use the county CSEA.** It exists in all 88 counties (R.C. 3125.10). It already:
- receives court orders;
- has enforcement staff, attorneys, notice systems, and administrative-hearing experience;
- is the body that would otherwise have to be invented.

A new state bureaucracy was rejected.

A court-housed office (the Michigan design) would be constitutionally easier, but it keeps the work
inside the courts. That is the opposite of the instruction.

### What the agency may do, power by power

| Power asked about | Answer | How |
|---|---|---|
| Initial determination that an unambiguous order was violated | **Yes** | § 3109.047(D). It binds only when the court enters it, which the court does if no one objects (the 3119.65 / 3111.49 model) |
| Direct equivalent make-up time | **Yes**, inside the determination | Same type and duration, within a year, at the wronged parent's choice on 7 days' notice (Michigan) |
| Administrative fee or civil penalty | **No penalty** | A penalty for disobeying a court order is contempt, which is judicial. Costs remain recoverable through contempt under (K)(1) |
| Require a conference | **Offer only** | A non-response is treated as showing no lawful excuse (a Michigan-style default), which works without compulsion |
| Initiate contempt for the parent | **Yes** | One phrase added to R.C. 2705.031(B)(2), mirroring the IV-D support rule in (B)(1) |
| Admissible findings | **Not legislated** | Evid.R. 803(8) controls (art. IV, § 5(B)). The record is filed; entered determinations are court orders |
| Expedited judicial review | **Yes** | Objection within 14 days; hearing within 28 days, decided anew |

### The triage

| Level | Who decides | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Routine** | The agency | Complaint → order verified → response → determination → make-up time |
| **2. Serious or repeated** | The agency refers | Three entered denials in 12 months, 14+ consecutive days, or concealment or removal from Ohio. The agency refers to the prosecutor and may file contempt |
| **3. Judicial** | The court | Unclear orders, good-faith safety claims, objections, contempt, and custody changes |

**One record per order.** It runs: order → complaint → response → determination → objection →
outcome → whether make-up time happened. It is filed in any later proceeding. That is the
standardized enforcement history the instruction asked for.

**Safety claims go straight to the court.** That stops the agency from deciding abuse questions. To
limit gaming:
- the claim must be made in good faith;
- the protective-action excuse requires a prompt report;
- a knowingly false allegation carries § 3109.046(G) consequences;
- the court hears it within 28 days.

## Part 3. Issue 2 — Prosecutor referral

The policy is unchanged:
- 60 days, with one self-executing extension of up to 30;
- either a charge or a written declination;
- no reasons required;
- not reviewable;
- no bar to a later prosecution or a private complaint.

Referral may now come from the agency (the usual route) or a court.

**Authority** [LAW]:
- *State ex rel. Master v. Cleveland*, 75 Ohio St.3d 23 (1996): "the decision whether to prosecute is
  discretionary, and not generally subject to judicial review."
- *State ex rel. Rankin v. State*, 2025-Ohio-4483: "a prosecutor has no clear duty to prosecute."
- Notice duties of the same type already exist: R.C. 2930.05(B)(2) and 2930.06.

**Placement.** The rule now sits in 2919.23(F).

## Part 4. Issue 3 — The restored parent's schedule motion

### What current Ohio law already allows [LAW]

- **Schedule changes need no change in circumstances.** *Braatz v. Braatz*, 85 Ohio St.3d 40 (1999):
  a parenting-time modification requires no change in circumstances, only best interest under R.C.
  3109.051(D).
- **Status changes do.** *Fisher v. Hasenjager*, 2007-Ohio-5589: changing the residential-parent
  designation requires a change in circumstances.

So the restored parent can already file a schedule motion. **The only question is the starting
point.**

### The practical thumb on the scale

Even with equal status, a best-interest hearing on an eight-year-old sole-custody schedule starts
from the status quo. The child's "adjustment to home, school, and community" (R.C. 3109.04(F)(1)(d))
favors whatever exists. Without a presumption, "equal status" changes the label, not the outcome.

### The options

| Option | Effect | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Presumption applies** to a schedule motion filed with or after restoration | The restored parent starts from 50/50 | Achieves the policy. Kentucky does this on modification [MODEL: KRS 403.340(5): "if the court orders a modification … there shall be a presumption, rebuttable by a preponderance of evidence, that it is in the best interest of the child for the parents to have joint custody and share equally in parenting time"]. Floodgate risk if unlimited, because Ohio has no change-in-circumstances filter for schedules (*Braatz*) |
| **B. No presumption; equal footing only** | Equality is stated, but the status quo still dominates through (F)(1)(d) | Symbolic. Fails the stated objective |
| **C. Presumption only within a window** (e.g., 24 months after enactment) | A surge of filings, then a cliff | Concentrates the floodgate rather than reducing it. Arbitrary for parents who learn of the law late |

**[REC] Option A, limited to the first schedule motion** filed with or after restoration.

- **Why it works.** The parent gets one real 50/50 hearing. Later motions return to ordinary best
  interest.
- **Why the floodgate is bounded:**
  - Restoration itself excludes violent parents.
  - The presumption is rebuttable on a preponderance. Real adjustment and stability evidence still
    counts, so a court can find it rebutted.
  - It is one motion per family, not serial relitigation.
  - The motion is already allowed (*Braatz*). The bill changes the starting point, not the right to
    file.
- **Why Kentucky's filter isn't copied.** Kentucky keeps a change-in-circumstances threshold before
  the presumption applies. Ohio does not have one for schedules, and adding one would make Ohio
  *less* open than today.
- **Retroactivity:** see Document 7, Part 1.
- **Honest cost.** This is the Ohio State Bar Association's floodgate argument, and it will be made.
  The answer is the one-motion limit and *Braatz*.

## Part 5. Issue 4 — Criminal interference

### What current law already does [LAW]

R.C. 2919.23(A)(1) reaches anyone who, "knowing the person is without privilege to do so or being
reckless in that regard," keeps a child from a parent. Three appellate districts apply it to parents
who withhold court-ordered time:
- *Toledo v. Parra*, 2013-Ohio-3182 (6th Dist.);
- *State v. Sprinkle*, 2007-Ohio-4967, ¶¶ 40–42 (12th Dist.), which applies it to a shared-parenting
  parent and quotes *Brickles* (2d Dist.): the statute "does not exclude parents with visitation from
  its reach";
- *State v. West*, 2002-Ohio-2114 (4th Dist.), which *Sprinkle* cites.

No contrary Ohio appellate decision was located.

### The options

| Option | Assessment |
|---|---|
| A. Revision 2: channel all withholding into a repeated-conduct offense | **Rejected.** It removes single-incident liability that three districts recognize, and adds an overlapping offense |
| B. Leave (A)(1) intact and add a separate repeated offense | Two offenses for one act. The allied-offense and election problems return. No added protection, because (A)(1) already reaches the repeated cases |
| **C. Leave (A)(1) intact; add three clarifying sentences** | **[REC]** Shortest. Weakens nothing |

### The three sentences, 2919.23(E)

1. **Withholding court-ordered time is keeping "without privilege,"** unless a listed lawful excuse
   applies. This codifies *Parra*/*Sprinkle* statewide, and the excuse list keeps accidents, illness,
   agreed changes, and protective action out.
2. **A reasonable reading of an unclear order is neither knowing nor reckless.** This is *Parra*
   ¶ 18's concern, and it addresses vagueness and notice.
3. **Not a civil matter.** *Parra* ¶¶ 17–18.

### Effect on the other concerns

- **"Serious, knowing, repeated":** this now governs *referral* (§ 3109.047(H)), not the elements of
  the crime. The agency refers serious cases; prosecutors decide (*Parra* ¶ 18: "may implicate
  prosecutorial discretion").
- **Mistakes:** an innocent mistake is neither knowing nor reckless (R.C. 2901.22), and the excuse
  list covers the rest.
- **Allied offenses:** none, because there is one offense.

## Part 6. Issue 5 — Only a court changes an order

The principle is kept as is.

**GAL [LAW]:**
- Sup.R. 8.06(A)(3): reports "shall not be considered determinative."
- Sup.R. 8.06(C)(3): the recommendation is considered "only when the report … has been admitted as an
  exhibit."
- *In re Hoffman*, 2002-Ohio-5368: a right to cross-examine the GAL, limited to permanent custody.

**[REC]** "A party may call the guardian ad litem as a witness and cross-examine." This fits Evid.R.
611 and Sup.R. 8.06(A)(4) (the GAL "shall be available to testify"), and extends *Hoffman* to
Chapter 3109 cases.

**Parenting coordinator [LAW]:**
- Sup.R. 6.42(B) already forbids parenting coordination for "[s]ubstantive changes in parenting
  time."
- *Gregory v. Gregory*, 2019-Ohio-5210: due process requires "meaningful and independent judicial
  review."

The bill codifies both. **Delegation case law** [LAW] is supportive but not settled:
- supportive: *Weisgarber*, 2009-Ohio-20; *Huffman*, 2002-Ohio-6031; *In re K.R.H.*,
  2026-Ohio-583;
- adverse, because the issue was not preserved: *Weinsziehr*, 2026-Ohio-2760.

That is the reason for a statute.

## Part 7. Issue 6 — The 36-month limit, stress-tested

### Continuance: 45 days, not 90 [REC]

- R.C. 2151.35(B)(1) allows one extension of "not … exceed forty-five days" for deciding abuse
  allegations [LAW].
- Revision 2 chose 90 to allow scheduling. Revision 3 adds a court-set hearing at 30 months, which
  gives six months' notice, so scheduling is no longer a reason.
- 45 is the tested Ohio number.

### Scenarios

| Scenario | What happens under § 3109.046 |
|---|---|
| A criminal case is pending | The custody court may decide anyway (§ 3109.046(C)). If the *restricted* parent asks to wait for the criminal case (for example, for Fifth Amendment reasons), that delay is not counted ((D)(3)). Speedy trial (R.C. 2945.71: 90 / 270 days) normally finishes the criminal case first anyway |
| A juvenile case is pending | The Chapter 2151 case is excluded from the section, but its adjudication resolves the allegation. It must be decided within 90 + 45 days or dismissed (R.C. 2151.35(B)(1)). The custody-court restriction that relies on it keeps its clock |
| A protection order exists | The protection order is excluded. A full-hearing finding resolves the allegation |
| The allegation is reworded | Every allegation about conduct before the restriction shares its period ((D)(4)). "However the allegation is later described" closes the repackaging loophole |
| Genuinely new misconduct | Conduct after the restriction began supports a new restriction with its own period |
| The restricted parent causes the delay | Not counted ((D)(3)), on the model of R.C. 2945.72(D), (H) speedy-trial tolling [LAW] |
| Due process needs more time | Either it is the restricted parent's own request (not counted), or the 45-day continuance, or the court rules within the period. Thirty-six months plus 45 days is ample for any civil fact-finding |
| Old conduct is disclosed late (for example, by the child) | It shares the existing period. The court can hear it within the period, so the clock does not restart |

## Part 8. Issue 7 — The 90-overnight floor: kept

**What the enacted states do [MODEL]:**
- Kentucky, West Virginia, Missouri, and Florida use "maximize" language with no numeric floor.
- Minnesota uses a numeric floor: "a rebuttable presumption that a child must receive a minimum of at
  least 25 percent of the parenting time with each parent" (Minn. Stat. 518.175 subd. 1(g)).

**Is "maximize" enough on its own?** No. "Maximize consistent with the child's welfare" is
unreviewable in practice. A court that rebuts 50/50 and orders every other weekend (about 52–80
overnights) can call that "maximized."

**Why keep a number, and why 90:**
- **It prevents marginalization.** The number makes the bottom reviewable.
- **It matches the tested floor.** 90 of 365 is 24.7 percent, which is Minnesota's 25 percent.
- **It is intuitive.** Ohio practitioners already count 90 overnights under R.C. 3119.051.

**Anchoring risk.** The floor could become the new normal. The risk is real but bounded:
- it applies only after rebuttal;
- the maximize duty and written findings still apply above it;
- the statute says in bold that it is "not a standard or target";
- Section 5(C)'s data will show any drift.

**Considered and rejected:** a percentage instead of overnights. It is less intuitive in Ohio.
Utah's hour-by-hour schedule is not restored.

## Part 9. Issue 8 — Workload

See Document 9. The design test applied: **"Does this eliminate a decision someone now has to ask a
judge to make?"**

**Added because they pass the test:**
- **Presumptive approval of agreed plans.** "[S]hall approve … unless it finds the agreement is not in
  the child's best interest." This flips current R.C. 3109.04(D)(1)(a)(i), which requires the court to
  find the plan *is* in the child's best interest.
- **Statewide forms.**
- **Agency enforcement.**
- **The three-strikes change in circumstances**, which now counts agency determinations too.

**Rejected because they add proceedings:**
- a separate expedited-enforcement motion;
- mandatory mediation;
- an agency appeal tier (objection goes straight to court).
