# DOCUMENT 4 — PLAIN-ENGLISH SUMMARY (REVISION 3)

## The Ohio Equal Parenting and Parent-Child Relationship Protection Act, in five minutes

*This describes a proposed bill. It is not law.*

### 1. Every legal parent is an equal parent.

Today, if you were never married to your child's other parent, Ohio makes the mother the sole legal
custodian until the father sues and wins. Under this bill, once paternity is legally established,
**both parents are equal legal custodians**. You don't have to sue.

**Equal status is not a schedule.** If there's no court order, either parent can ask a court for one.
If a temporary order is made without a hearing, you can get a hearing within 28 days.

### 2. The starting point is 50/50.

When a court first decides custody, it starts from **shared parenting with substantially equal
time**, meaning at least 45 percent each. Either parent can show that something different is better
for the child, using the ordinary "more likely than not" standard. No criminal conviction is needed
to protect a child.

The starting point **doesn't apply** to a parent under a protection order, or to a parent convicted
of abusing a family member or found responsible for it. If the parents agree on a plan, the judge
approves it unless it's bad for the child.

**Not enough by themselves** to defeat 50/50:
- the parents don't get along;
- one parent objects;
- one parent refuses to cooperate;
- an accusation the court hasn't found true.

If the judge orders less than equal time, the judge must **explain why in writing** and give each
parent as much time as the child's welfare allows. **No parent gets fewer than 90 overnights a year**
(about a quarter of the year) unless:
- the parents agree;
- that parent asks for less;
- distance makes it impossible; or
- more time would endanger the child.

**Ninety is a floor, not a goal.** Temporary orders can't quietly become permanent.

### 3. If you already have an old custody order, you can become an equal parent again.

File a motion. You **don't** have to prove that circumstances changed. The judge must grant it unless
you have a proven history of physical violence or abuse, or you're an immediate threat.

**This restores your status, not your schedule.** Your current schedule, child support, and the
child's school stay exactly as they are until a judge changes them. If your order gives you no time,
you still have no time, and you can't just take the child.

What changes is this: when you ask the judge for more time, **your first request starts from 50/50**,
just like a new case.

### 4. Only a judge can change a court order.

If the order says Tuesday 4 to 7, it's Tuesday 4 to 7 until a judge changes it. Nobody else can change
it, suspend it, or add conditions to it: not the other parent, a guardian ad litem (GAL), a parenting
coordinator, a counselor, a caseworker, or a police officer. Parents can still agree to swap a day.

A **GAL** investigates and recommends, and you can question the GAL in court. A GAL's recommendation
changes nothing until a judge signs an order. A children-services "safety plan" can't cut your court
time unless you agree in writing or a judge orders it. Real emergencies are still handled through
protection orders and emergency custody.

### 5. Missed time: a county office handles it, not a lawsuit.

Your county **child support enforcement agency** becomes the parenting time enforcement agency.
1. **File a complaint** on a simple form within 60 days of the missed time. There's no fee.
2. The agency gets the court order, asks the other parent to respond within 14 days, and decides
   within 45 days.
3. If the order is clear and there's no valid excuse, the agency **sets make-up time** of the same
   kind and length. You pick the times, with a week's notice, within a year.
4. Either parent can object to a judge within 14 days, and the judge hears it within 28 days. If
   nobody objects, the judge signs it, and it becomes an enforceable court order.

**Valid excuses:**
- a real emergency, illness, weather, or car trouble;
- a change you both agreed to;
- following a protection order;
- a good-faith effort to protect the child that is promptly reported.

**If the child refuses:** you must have the child ready, tell the child about the visit, not
discourage it, and let the other parent know. You never have to use force.

If someone claims the visit would endanger the child, or the order is unclear, the agency **sends it
straight to the judge**.

The agency keeps **one record** of every complaint and outcome for your order, so a judge doesn't have
to piece it together later. **Three violations within two years** are grounds to change custody.

### 6. Accusations must be decided, not left hanging.

An accusation alone changes nothing. A judge must order any restriction, based on evidence.

If your time is restricted because of an accusation, you can demand a hearing within 90 days. An open
investigation or a pending criminal case doesn't stop the judge from deciding.

**Outside limit:**
- A restriction based on an undecided accusation **ends after 36 months**.
- The judge must set a hearing at 30 months.
- The judge can extend once, by up to 45 days.
- Delays you cause don't count.
- **The same old accusation can't be reworded to restart the clock.**

**Honest reports are protected.** "Unsubstantiated," "not charged," "dismissed," or "mistaken" does
not mean "false." Only a **knowingly false** accusation made to cut the other parent out has
consequences: make-up time, the other parent's legal fees, and grounds to change custody.

### 7. Serious withholding is a crime, and it's not "just civil."

Ohio already makes it a crime to keep a child from a parent without privilege (R.C. 2919.23).
Appeals courts have applied it to parents who withhold court-ordered time. The bill says so plainly:
- Keeping a child during the other parent's court-ordered time **is** keeping without privilege,
  unless one of the valid excuses applies.
- An honest reading of an unclear order isn't a crime.
- **It is not "a civil matter."** Police can investigate and prosecutors can prosecute, whether or
  not there's also a contempt case.

**When the agency refers a case:** after three violations in a year, a withholding of 14 days or
more, or a child hidden or taken out of Ohio, the agency refers the case to the prosecutor. The
prosecutor decides whether to charge. Within 60 days (90 with one extension), the prosecutor must
either file charges or send written notice of declining. No reasons are required.

### 8. What this bill does not do.

- It doesn't change how child support is calculated.
- It doesn't automatically rewrite anyone's schedule.
- It doesn't punish honest reports of abuse.
- It doesn't use "parental alienation" or any psychological theory.
- It doesn't take away a judge's power to protect a child.
