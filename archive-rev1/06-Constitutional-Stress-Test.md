## 1. The presumptions — § 3109.086(A) and (B)

**The attack.** "*Troxel v. Granville*, 530 U.S. 57 (2000), holds that the interest of
parents in the care, custody and control of their children is 'perhaps the oldest of the
fundamental liberty interests,' and that there is a presumption that fit parents act in
their children's best interests. A statute that presumes a *particular schedule* is in the
child's best interest overrides each fit parent's own judgment and replaces individualized
determination with a legislative rule. It also burdens the parent who must now prove a
negative."

**Why this fails.**

- *Troxel* protects parents **against the state**, in a dispute between a parent and a
  non-parent. § 3109.086 operates in a dispute **between two parents**, both of whom hold
  the same fundamental interest. The state is not displacing parental judgment in favour of
  its own; it is allocating a burden between two equally situated rights-holders. *Troxel*
  supplies no rule for that, and *Harrold v. Collier*, 107 Ohio St.3d 44, 2005-Ohio-5334,
  confirms that even the *Troxel* presumption itself is rebuttable.
- A presumption is a burden-allocation device, not an outcome. It is rebuttable on the
  lowest civil standard, on ten enumerated grounds plus a catch-all, and the court retains
  every safety power it has.
- **The decisive point is precedential.** Four states have enacted materially identical
  presumptions — Kentucky (2018), Arkansas (2021), West Virginia (2022), Florida (2023) —
  and this research located no decision striking any of them on substantive due process
  grounds. Kentucky's has operated for eight years and was *strengthened* rather than
  repealed in 2026.
- § 3109.086(D)(1) removes the presumption entirely where the parents agree, so the statute
  never overrides a joint parental judgment. That is the precise *Troxel* concern, and it is
  answered on the face of the section.

**Fixes already in the draft.** § 3109.086(D)(1) (agreement carve-out); § 3109.086(H)
(savings clause); § 3109.084(C) (express codification of the fit-parent presumption, which
converts the strongest attack into a provision the bill itself contains).

**Residual risk: low.** The presumption is the *best*-defended provision in the bill.

## 2. Equal parental status for parents who were never married — § 3109.085

**The attack.** "Replacing a settled rule disrupts reliance interests; and conferring status
automatically on a man who has done nothing but sign an acknowledgment devalues the mother's
established caregiving."

**Why this fails, and why the real exposure runs the other way.**

The provision under constitutional stress here is **not** § 3109.085. It is
**current R.C. 3109.042(A)** and its successor, **S.B. 174 proposed R.C. 3109.0424**. Those
provisions assign sole legal custody of a child to one parent, by operation of law, on two
classifications:

- **Marital status** of the parents — a married father and an unmarried father, identically
  situated as to parentage, fitness and conduct, receive categorically different legal
  status; and
- **Sex** in practical operation — the rule is written to apply to "an unmarried female who
  gives birth," and the burdened class is necessarily male.

Sex-based classifications in the law of parentage have been repeatedly narrowed by the
Supreme Court, and Ohio's own statute already concedes the underlying principle in its
second sentence, which commands that a court "shall treat the mother and father as standing
upon an equality." The statute thus asserts equality as the governing norm while denying it
until one parent litigates.

Note what § 3109.085 does **not** do. It does not award custody, a schedule, or a residence
to anybody. It confers *status* — standing as a parent — and expressly separates that from
the schedule in division (C). The interim schedule in division (D) is the statutory
*minimum*, not equal time, and it is subject to four exceptions including an ex parte
immediate-risk finding. A mother's caregiving history remains fully available as evidence in
any allocation proceeding, including under S.B. 174 proposed R.C. 3109.0430(A)(3) and (14).

**Fixes already in the draft.** Division (C) (status/schedule separation); division (D)
(minimum, not equal, as the interim rule); division (E) (protection order, rape/sexual
battery, immediate risk, each parent-specific); division (F) (sex-neutral rape and sexual
battery exclusion, curing the sex-specific drafting of current R.C. 3109.042(B)).

**Residual risk: low.** This provision **reduces** the state's aggregate constitutional
exposure. That should be said affirmatively in sponsor testimony; it is a stronger argument
than the fairness argument and it has not been made in any prior Ohio bill.

## 3. The statutory minimum schedule — § 3109.087

**The attack.** "A legislated hour-by-hour schedule is the antithesis of individualized
best-interest determination. A court confronted with a parent whose circumstances make even
the minimum inappropriate — an unstable but not provably dangerous parent, a newborn, a
parent who has not seen the child in five years — is forbidden to order less unless it can
make an abuse or domestic-violence finding. The floor will produce orders that are wrong for
particular children."

**This attack has real force and is only partly answered by drafting.** As drafted,
§ 3109.087(C) permits departure below the floor **only** on a finding that a restriction is
reasonably calculated to protect against abuse or domestic violence. There are cases that
fit neither description and in which the floor is nonetheless wrong: the long-absent parent
for whom a graduated reintroduction is obviously indicated; the newborn; the parent whose
serious mental illness is not "abuse."

**Fix 1 — required.** Broaden § 3109.087(C)(1) to track the substance of S.B. 174 proposed
R.C. 3109.0456's grounds rather than only proposed R.C. 3109.0455's abuse-and-DV formula.
Recommended replacement:

> **(C)(1)** The court finds, based upon a preponderance of the evidence, that a restriction
> is reasonably calculated to protect the child from physical, sexual, or emotional abuse, to
> protect a parent from domestic violence, **or that one or more of the circumstances
> described in divisions (A) to (I) of section 3109.0456 of the Revised Code applies and the
> minimum parenting time schedule would endanger the child's physical or emotional
> welfare**; and

**Fix 2 — required.** Add a graduated-reintroduction provision. Without it the floor is
unworkable in the case it will most often meet:

> **(G)** Where a parent has not exercised parenting time with the child for a substantial
> period, the court may order a graduated schedule that reaches the minimum parenting time
> schedule over a period stated in the order, not exceeding one year. An order under this
> division is not a departure under division (C) of this section.

**Fix 3 — recommended.** Add an express agreement carve-out, parallel to § 3109.086(D)(1):

> **(H)** This section does not apply where the parents have agreed to a parenting time
> schedule and the court approves the agreement.

With those three fixes, the floor becomes what the brief intends — an anti-arbitrariness
rule, not a mandate — and the "wrong for particular children" attack loses its examples.

**Residual risk after fixes: low-moderate.** This remains the most novel provision in the
bill (no state combines a 50/50 presumption with a statutory floor), which is a political
exposure more than a legal one.

## 4. Speech-based acts — § 3109.088(B)(5), (6), (7)

**This is the highest-risk area in the bill and it is worth being blunt about why.**

**The attack.** "Division (B)(5) reaches 'knowingly encouraging … the child to refuse'
parenting time — pure parent-child speech. Divisions (B)(6) and (7) reach statements. Parents
have a First Amendment right to speak to their own children, including critically about the
other parent. *Shak v. Shak*, 484 Mass. 658 (2020), struck a non-disparagement order in a
custody case as an unconstitutional prior restraint. Making speech a predicate for a *crime*
and for loss of custody will chill parents from saying true things and from warning children
about genuine risks."

**What survives and what does not.**

- **(B)(6) and (B)(7) are defensible** *because of* § 3109.0882(C), which requires that the
  statement be (i) materially false as to **fact**, (ii) **known** to be false, and (iii) made
  **for the purpose** of preventing lawful parenting time. Knowingly false statements of fact
  occupy the lowest tier of First Amendment protection, and speech that is an integral part of
  a course of unlawful conduct is unprotected. Without § 3109.0882(C), (B)(6) and (B)(7) would
  be indefensible; with it, they are narrow.
- **(B)(5) is the genuinely exposed provision.** "Encouraging" a child is speech, and a
  reasonable parent cannot always tell where sympathy ends and encouragement begins. A parent
  who says "I know you're scared, I'll tell your father you don't want to come" has arguably
  encouraged refusal.

**Fix 4 — required.** Narrow (B)(5) to *direction* coupled with the parent's own
non-compliance, so that the predicate act is conduct with a speech component rather than
speech alone:

> **(B)(5)** Directing the child to refuse court-ordered parenting time, or knowingly
> encouraging the child to refuse court-ordered parenting time, where the person also fails to
> make reasonable efforts under division (A) of section 3109.089 of the Revised Code, and
> where the person acts for the purpose of interfering with the child's relationship with the
> other parent;

This means no liability attaches to a parent who speaks sympathetically but still makes
reasonable efforts to deliver the child. It restores the requirement that something be *done*,
not merely *said*.

**Fix 5 — required.** Add an express speech savings clause. It costs nothing and it is the
provision an appellate court will look for:

> **(G)** Nothing in this section prohibits, or imposes liability for, a parent's expression
> of opinion, criticism, or negative statements regarding the other parent, or a parent's
> truthful statement to the child or to any other person. This section does not authorize a
> court to prohibit a parent from speaking about the other parent.

**Fix 6 — recommended.** In R.C. 2919.232, exclude the speech-only acts from criminal reach
entirely. Criminal liability should require conduct: denial, prevention of exchange,
concealment, removal. Recommended addition:

> **(I)** No person shall be convicted under this section on the basis of conduct described in
> division (B)(5), (B)(6), or (B)(7) of section 3109.088 of the Revised Code alone.

This is a real narrowing and a real policy cost: a parent who lies to police to stop an
exchange would face civil consequences but not this offence (though R.C. 2921.13 and 2921.14
remain available). It is recommended anyway. The criminal provision is the part of this bill
most likely to be challenged, and it should be the part with the least speech content.

**Residual risk after fixes: low-moderate.** The remaining exposure is the possibility of
as-applied challenges, which is unavoidable and manageable.

## 5. The criminal offence — R.C. 2919.232

**Attack A — vagueness.** "'Course of conduct,' 'substantially interferes' and 'without
lawful justification' give neither notice nor enforcement guidance."

**Answer.** Each term is anchored. "Course of conduct" is **numerically defined** in (B) —
three denials in 90 days, or 30 consecutive days. "Substantially interferes" is defined by the
same division. "Without lawful justification" is **enumerated** in (C), with six specific
justifications, and reinforced by six express exclusions in (D). The reference point is an
existing court order, which the defendant has been served with. This is materially more
definite than R.C. 2919.23, which has long been enforced on the considerably vaguer "entice,
take, keep, or harbor" - a phrase *Toledo v. Parra* had to construe to reach a custodial
parent who simply stood still.

**Attack B — mens rea and burden.** "The three-denial trigger will be used to presume intent
from the count, which *Sandstrom v. Montana* and *Francis v. Franklin* forbid."

**Answer, and a necessary fix.** § 3109.088(E) already states that division (D) "does not
establish a presumption, and it does not relieve a party or the state of the burden of
proving any element." But § 3109.088(E) sits in Chapter 3109 and the offence sits in Chapter
2919, and a criminal court should not have to reach across chapters for it.

**Fix 7 — required.** State it inside the offence:

> **(J)** The state shall prove each element of this offense beyond a reasonable doubt. No
> element of this offense, including the offender's knowledge or purpose, may be presumed or
> inferred as a matter of law from proof of the number or duration of periods of parenting
> time denied.

Note also that (C) as drafted already places the burden of disproving lawful justification on
the **state** rather than making it an affirmative defence. That is more protective than
R.C. 2919.23(C) and more protective than Minn. Stat. § 609.26, subd. 2. It is deliberate. The
brief asks which convention Ohio drafting uses: R.C. 2901.05(D) defines affirmative defences,
and Ohio commonly uses them — R.C. 2919.23(C) is an example. **The recommendation is to keep
the state's burden for (C)(1) through (C)(5) and consider making only (C)(6) (the other
parent's agreement) an affirmative defence**, because agreement is a fact peculiarly within
the defendant's knowledge and is the justification most susceptible to fabrication. That
allocation is defensible under *Patterson v. New York* and matches how Ohio treats consent
elsewhere.

**Attack C — double jeopardy and allied offences.** Conduct may violate R.C. 2919.232,
R.C. 2919.23, and R.C. 2921.14. Division (H) addresses election between 2919.232 and 2919.23.
**Fix 8 — recommended:** add an allied-offence direction so the issue is resolved at
sentencing rather than on appeal:

> **(K)** If the same conduct constitutes a violation of this section and of section 2919.23
> of the Revised Code, the offender may be convicted of only one of those offenses.

**Attack D — due process where the underlying order is ambiguous.** *Parra* itself flagged
this at ¶ 18: proving knowledge of absence of privilege "based on the interpretation of the
order of another court may implicate prosecutorial discretion." A criminal conviction should
not turn on a contestable reading of a domestic relations order.

**Fix 9 — required.** This is the single most important fix in this document:

> **(L)** No person shall be convicted under this section unless the provision of the court
> order that the person is alleged to have violated is definite and specific as to the
> periods of parenting time allocated. A person does not violate this section by acting on a
> reasonable interpretation of an ambiguous order.

This does substantial work. It confines the offence to clear orders — which is where the
conduct is genuinely culpable — and it dovetails with R.C. 3109.051(A)'s existing requirement
that the court "include in its final decree a specific schedule of parenting time."

**Residual risk after fixes: low-moderate.**

## 6. The duty to facilitate — § 3109.089

**The attack.** "Requiring a parent to 'make reasonable efforts to ensure that the child …
complies' compels the parent to advocate the state's position to their own child. And
'reasonable efforts' is undefined, so a parent cannot know what satisfies it."

**Answer.** Division (C) already provides that reasonable efforts "do not require a parent to
use physical force," which removes the worst case. But the compelled-speech objection is not
fully answered, and "reasonable efforts" needs a floor.

**Fix 10 — recommended.** Define the minimum conduct that satisfies the duty, so that the
standard is a safe harbour rather than an open-ended command:

> **(F)** A parent satisfies division (A) of this section by doing all of the following:
> making the child available at the time and place required by the order; informing the child
> of the scheduled parenting time; not discouraging the child from exercising it; and promptly
> notifying the other parent if the child refuses. Division (A) does not require a parent to
> use physical force, to make any particular statement, or to express any particular view.

The final clause is the compelled-speech fix. With it, the duty is a duty of *availability and
non-discouragement*, not of persuasion.

**Residual risk after fix: low.**

## 7. The reverse presumption — § 3109.086(G)

**The attack.** "A finding of two acts of domestic violence, possibly made on a preponderance
in a civil proceeding years earlier, strips a parent of the presumption."

**Answer.** This is KRS 403.315(2), enacted and effective in Kentucky. It is rebuttable, on
the same preponderance standard. It requires a **court finding**, not an allegation —
§ 3109.0871(C) makes that explicit. It does not bar parenting time; it allocates a burden. And
because it operates only on a finding, the parent had notice and an opportunity to be heard in
the proceeding that produced it.

**Residual risk: low.** Consider, for completeness, whether the finding must have been made
in a proceeding in which the affected parent was a party and had counsel or the opportunity to
obtain it. A one-line addition would foreclose the point.

## 8. Temporary orders — § 3109.0872

**The attack.** "Applying a presumption to an ex parte order issued on affidavit without a
hearing decides a fundamental-rights question without process."

**Answer.** The presumption *favours* both parents equally, so applying it at the temporary
stage **increases** the protection of the parent who would otherwise be excluded pending a
hearing. Division (D) guarantees an oral hearing within 28 days of request — that is
S.B. 174's own number, unchanged. Division (C) guarantees that nothing decided at the
temporary stage carries forward. Division (E) prevents indefinite interim status.

**Residual risk: low.** Division (C) is the provision that makes the whole Part safe, because
it means the temporary order carries no preclusive weight.

## 9. The burden shift — § 3109.0881(C)

**The attack.** "Shifting the burden to the responding parent on proof of an order and a
missed period presumes wrongdoing from absence."

**Answer.** It shifts the **burden of producing evidence**, not the burden of persuasion, and
only as to lawful justification — a matter peculiarly within the responding parent's
knowledge. This is ordinary civil practice. Illinois (750 ILCS 5/607.5) and Arizona
(A.R.S. § 25-414) operate comparable expedited schemes. § 3109.0881(E) further protects the
responding parent by excluding inadvertent, minimal, cured, and agreed changes.

**Fix 11 — recommended.** Say "burden of producing evidence" in the text rather than
"burden," to foreclose any argument that the burden of persuasion moved.

**Residual risk: low.**

## 10. Retroactivity — Section 3

Ohio Constitution, Article II, Section 28: "The general assembly shall have no power to pass
retroactive laws." The Act is prospective by design. Section 3(A) limits it to proceedings
commenced and initial allocations made on or after the effective date; (B) preserves prior
orders; (C) states that enactment is not itself a change in circumstances.

Section 3(D) applies the enforcement provisions and R.C. 2919.232 to **conduct** occurring on
or after the effective date, whatever the date of the order violated. **This is not
retroactive.** The operative event is the conduct, not the order. Compare R.C. 2919.23, which
has always applied to violations of pre-existing orders. No ex post facto problem arises for
the same reason.

Florida's silence on this question generated appellate litigation over whether its 2023
presumption applied to pre-2023 orders. Ohio should not repeat that.

**Residual risk: low.**

## 11. Local-rule conformity — § 3109.087(E)

**The attack.** "Ohio Constitution, Article IV, Section 5(B) vests rulemaking in the Supreme
Court, and courts of common pleas adopt local rules under that authority. A statute commanding
courts to conform their local rules to legislated content invades the judicial power."

**This is the most legally serious of the separation-of-powers objections and deserves a
careful answer.**

Ohio's rule is that the Supreme Court's rulemaking power reaches **practice and procedure**,
while **substantive law** is the General Assembly's. Article IV, Section 5(B) itself provides
that rules "shall not abridge, enlarge, or modify any substantive right." A minimum quantum of
parenting time is substantive, not procedural — it defines a parent's entitlement, not the
manner of asserting it. And the General Assembly has already legislated in precisely this
space: **R.C. 3109.051(F)(2) is itself a statutory command to courts of common pleas to adopt
parenting-time guidelines by rule**, dating from the 1990-91 shared-parenting reforms and
apparently never successfully challenged on this ground. § 3109.087(E) adds content to an
existing statutory mandate; it does not create a new one. *(This research did not locate any
reported challenge to R.C. 3109.051(F)(2); that absence is suggestive, not conclusive, and
should be confirmed before the point is made in testimony.)*

Note also that S.B. 174 Section 4 takes the *softer* route — it "requests" local-rule review
rather than requiring conformity. That drafting choice was presumably deliberate and reflects
institutional sensitivity.

**Fix 12 — recommended.** Reduce the exposure without losing the substance by separating the
two functions. Make the **floor** a substantive rule binding on courts in their adjudicative
capacity (§ 3109.087(A) and (C) already do this, and need no local rule to operate), and make
the **local-rule conformity** provision a request rather than a command:

> **(E)** The general assembly requests that each court of common pleas conform the standard
> parenting time guidelines adopted by rule under division (F)(2) of section 3109.051 of the
> Revised Code to the minimum parenting time schedule established by division (B) of this
> section. A court may adopt standard parenting time guidelines that provide more parenting
> time than the minimum parenting time schedule. **Regardless of the content of any rule
> adopted under division (F)(2) of section 3109.051 of the Revised Code, a court shall not
> order less parenting time than the minimum parenting time schedule except as provided in
> division (C) of this section.**

The final sentence preserves the entire policy — a non-conforming local rule simply cannot be
applied to order less than the floor — while the request in the first sentence removes the
separation-of-powers target. **This fix loses nothing and should be adopted.** The parallel
amendment to R.C. 3109.051(F)(2) in Document 4 Part II should be softened the same way.

**Residual risk after fix: low.**

## 12. Single subject — Ohio Const. art. II, § 15(D)

"No bill shall contain more than one subject, which shall be clearly expressed in its title."

The Act contains domestic-relations provisions (Chapter 3109), a criminal provision (Chapter
2919), a parentage amendment (R.C. 3111.13), and uncodified child-support and data sections. The
common subject — the allocation, protection and enforcement of parental rights and parenting
time — is unitary and is expressed in the title.

The single-subject rule is enforced only against **manifestly gross and fraudulent** violations,
and Ohio has repeatedly sustained omnibus legislation far more heterogeneous than this. S.B. 174
itself amends 105 sections across a dozen chapters including Title 29 offences, education,
insurance, and higher education. This Act is comparatively tidy.

**Residual risk: low.**

## 13. Two risks that are not constitutional

**A. The floor becomes the ceiling.** The most likely real-world failure mode of this Act is
not invalidation. It is that § 3109.087(B) becomes the schedule courts order, because it is the
number the legislature wrote down. § 3109.087(D) is drafted against exactly this, and
§ 3109.0863 requires the court to reach the floor by *maximizing* rather than by defaulting. The
only real safeguard, however, is measurement — Section 5's data report is what would reveal the
drift, and it is the reason that section is in the bill rather than being dropped as
decorative.

**B. Evidentiary escalation.** The Ohio State Bar Association's costliest objection to H.B. 14 —
$5,000–$10,000 psychological evaluations, $1,500–$2,000 guardians ad litem, continuous alcohol
monitoring — was driven by the **clear and convincing** standard, which is why this draft uses
preponderance. But any presumption raises the stakes of rebuttal to some degree. Two mitigations
are worth considering:
- Direct that the court may rebut the presumption on the evidence before it without ordering an
  evaluation, and that failure to obtain an evaluation is not a failure of proof.
- Confirm that R.C. 3109.051(L) fee-waiver relief and existing indigency provisions apply to
  proceedings under the Act.

Neither is constitutionally required. Both answer the objection that actually killed the last
bill.

One consequential drafting decision was made in the course of incorporating Fix 1. Division (I)
reproduces divisions (A) to (I) of S.B. 174 proposed R.C. 3109.0456 but **omits its division
(J)**, "[a]ny other relevant factor that affects the best interest of a child." An open-ended
catch-all is appropriate for authorizing discretionary restrictions, which is what
R.C. 3109.0456 does. It is not appropriate for authorizing a departure below a statutory floor,
because a floor with a catch-all exception is not a floor. If that omission is reversed, Fix 1
loses most of its value and § 3109.087 loses most of its force.

**Residual assessment after incorporation.** The three highest-value fixes were 9, 5 and 7.
Together they convert R.C. 2919.232 from a provision a court might strike into one it would
narrow at worst: the offence now reaches only definite orders, excludes speech-only conduct,
protects expression expressly, and forbids presuming any element from the denial count. The
bill's remaining exposure is concentrated in two places — the novelty of combining a 50/50
presumption with a statutory floor (a political rather than legal exposure), and the
possibility of as-applied First Amendment challenges to § 3109.088(B)(6) and (7), which is
unavoidable and manageable.
