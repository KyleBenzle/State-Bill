# DOCUMENT 3 — WHAT THIS BILL DOES

### Ohio Equal Parenting and Parent-Child Relationship Protection Act
*One page, plain English.*

---

**The starting point becomes equal.** Today an Ohio court picks one parent to be the
"residential parent and legal custodian" unless a parent files a shared parenting plan.
This bill says that when parents live apart, a court starts from equal parenting time and
shared major decisions. A parent who wants a different arrangement has to show, by the
ordinary standard used in every other family law question — more likely than not — that
equal time is not in the child's best interest.

**Married or not makes no difference.** Today, if parents were never married, the mother is
the sole legal custodian by operation of law until a father goes to court and wins. This
bill ends that. Once parentage is legally established — by a final acknowledgment of
paternity, a court order, or adoption — both parents have equal legal status automatically.
No separate lawsuit is needed to become an ordinary parent. Until the parents get a
schedule from a court, the minimum schedule below applies, so the child is not left in
limbo.

**If equal time is rejected, the court does not fall back to every other weekend.** It has
to build the schedule that gives each parent as much time as possible while keeping the
child safe. That is the current law in West Virginia, Kentucky, and Canada.

**There is a floor.** Even when equal time is rejected, a fit parent ordinarily gets no
less than a statewide minimum schedule: one weekday evening a week, alternating weekends,
alternating holidays, up to four weeks in the summer, and reasonable phone and video
contact. This is not the goal — it is the bottom. It is roughly the least generous schedule
Ohio counties already publish, and Utah has had a schedule like it in statute for years.

**A judge can always go lower to protect a child.** If the court finds — more likely than
not, and a criminal conviction is *not* required — that a restriction is needed to protect
a child from abuse or a parent from domestic violence, it can order less time, supervised
time, or no time. It has to use the least restrictive option that actually works. Nothing
in this bill makes a judge leave a child somewhere dangerous.

**Abuse gets looked at first, not last.** Before weighing anything else, the court has to
address any allegation of domestic violence, child abuse, or neglect and say in writing what
effect it has on the child. If one parent has committed two or more acts of domestic
violence, the presumption flips against that parent. If a parent is protected by a domestic
violence order, the presumption does not apply to the parent it was entered against. And a
parent who resists contact because the other parent abused them cannot have that held
against them. All of this is taken from Kentucky law as amended in 2026.

**The equal-time starting point applies from day one.** It applies to temporary orders too,
so a parent is not reduced to a visitor for the two or three years a case takes. And the
length of a temporary order can never be used as a reason to make it permanent.

**Judges write two sentences, not an opinion.** If a court orders substantially less than
equal time over a parent's objection, it states the facts that overcame the presumption and
why the schedule it chose is necessary for the child. That is all. That is less paperwork
than current law already requires when a parent asks for findings.

**Denying court-ordered parenting time finally has consequences that follow a pattern.**
File a motion, get a hearing within 28 days. Show the order and the missed time and you have
made your case; the other parent then has to justify it. Then:

- **First proven unjustified denial** — make-up time, same type and length, within six
  months. Mandatory, not optional. Plus a warning.
- **Second and after** — make-up time plus at least one real consequence: costs and
  attorney fees, a civil sanction up to $500 per missed period, parent education, supervised
  exchanges, or a bond.
- **Three or more findings** — double make-up time, fees that cannot be waived, and the
  pattern counts as a change in circumstances, which means the court can revisit who the
  child lives with. This is Texas law as of September 2025.

Honest mistakes are not violations. A denial that was accidental, brief, promptly fixed, or
agreed to is not sanctioned beyond make-up time.

**"Parental alienation" is described by what a person actually does.** The bill does not use
that phrase and does not require anyone to prove a psychological syndrome — Brazil tried
that in 2010 and the United Nations has asked Brazil to repeal it, because it got used
against mothers reporting abuse. Instead the bill names nine specific acts: denying ordered
time, blocking exchanges, hiding the child, telling a child to refuse, lying to a court or
to police to stop parenting time, withholding required medical or school information as part
of a pattern, and moving or concealing the child to defeat an order. Every one of them is
something you either did or did not do.

**Reporting abuse in good faith is fully protected — and protected both ways.** A report
that turns out to be unsubstantiated, unproven, not prosecuted, or simply mistaken is not a
violation of anything, and cannot be counted against the parent who made it. Only a
statement the person *knew* was false, that mattered, and that was made *for the purpose* of
blocking parenting time can ever qualify. A parent who reports promptly and in good faith
and keeps the child home while the agency responds has a complete defense.

**Serious, repeated, deliberate interference can be a crime — narrowly.** Three knowing,
unjustified denials in 90 days, or a single deliberate deprivation of 30 days or more, by a
person who has custody or control of the child, is a first-degree misdemeanor. It becomes a
fifth-degree felony for a repeat offender, or if the child was hidden, taken out of Ohio,
or if there were threats or violence, or if the deprivation ran past 90 days. Accidents,
misunderstandings, agreed changes, illness, weather, things outside your control, and
reasonable protective action are all excluded by name. Ohio courts already allow these
prosecutions — *Toledo v. Parra*, 2013 — but the existing statute was written for child
stealing, so prosecutors decline. This one is written for what actually happens, and it says
plainly that having a contempt remedy available does not make the conduct "just a civil
matter."

**"The child doesn't want to go" is not a free pass.** A parent has to make reasonable
efforts to get the child to court-ordered time and tell the other parent promptly if it
fails. Reasonable efforts never require physical force. A mature child's views still matter
and the court still hears them — the bill changes nothing about that.

**Nobody's existing order is reopened.** The bill applies going forward to new cases. Orders
already in place stay in place, and passing this bill is not by itself a reason to go back to
court. The one exception is enforcement: if you violate an existing order after the effective
date, the new enforcement rules apply to you.

**Child support is not touched.** Equal parenting time does not by itself mean no child
support, because incomes differ. The schedule, the worksheet, and every deviation rule stay
exactly as they are.

**Someone will finally count.** The Supreme Court of Ohio is asked to publish annual numbers
on what schedules courts actually order, how often the presumption is rebutted and why, and
how enforcement motions come out. Right now nobody in Ohio knows.

### The whole bill in six lines

1. Start at 50/50 — for temporary orders too.
2. Married or not, both legal parents start equal.
3. If 50/50 is beaten, maximize both parents' time — never default to every other weekend.
4. There is a statewide floor, and it is a floor, not a target.
5. Safety always wins, on ordinary proof, and abuse is examined first.
6. Denying court-ordered time costs something, and it costs more each time.
