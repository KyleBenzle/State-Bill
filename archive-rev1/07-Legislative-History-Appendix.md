## PART 1 — H.B. 508, 134th GENERAL ASSEMBLY (2021–2022)

**Status: did not pass.**

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Primary sponsors | Reps. West and Creech |
| Committee | House Civil Justice |
| Outcome | Died in committee; not reported |

### What it contained

- A statement of state policy: minor children should have a continuing, full parent-and-child
  relationship with their parents, and parents should share equally in parenting time and the
  rights and responsibilities of child rearing, after legal separation, divorce, dissolution or
  annulment "or in situations where the mother is unmarried."
- A rebuttable presumption that equal decision-making rights and responsibilities and equal
  parenting time are in the child's best interest, rebuttable by **clear and convincing
  evidence**.
- A requirement that the court issue a **shared parenting order** if the presumptions were not
  rebutted.
- A factor list for determining rebuttal (parents' ability to cooperate; history of abuse or
  domestic violence; the child's wishes; others).
- **A requirement that each court of common pleas adopt rules on standard parenting time
  guidelines "as established in the bill."**
- **A requirement that each court compile an annual report of data regarding the division of
  parenting rights and responsibilities.**

### Why it matters to this project

Two provisions of H.B. 508 were dropped from H.B. 14 and are **revived in this proposal**:

1. **The statewide guidelines requirement.** H.B. 508 is the Ohio origin of the Part IX floor
   concept. It used the existing R.C. 3109.051(F)(2) local-rule mandate as the delivery
   mechanism — the same mechanism § 3109.087(E) uses. This matters for testimony: the floor is
   not an out-of-state import layered onto Ohio law. It is an Ohio idea that has been in an Ohio
   bill before, delivered through an Ohio statute that has existed since 1990.
2. **The data report.** Revived as Section 5. In 2026, National Parents Organization asked the
   House Judiciary Committee for exactly this, calling for "a requirement to generate and
   evaluate metrics for success" and identifying three measures: settlement rates and litigation
   cost, frequency of substantially equal schedules, and family violence rates. S.B. 174 contains
   no data provision.

### Key operative provisions

**Policy — proposed R.C. 3109.044.** Quoted in full in Document 1 § C.5. Three divisions:
frequent associations and a continuing relationship with both parents; encouragement of shared
rights and responsibilities; and "[t]hat, to the greatest degree possible, parents share equally
in parenting time and the rights and responsibilities of rearing their children."

**Dual presumption — proposed R.C. 3109.0420.** Applied only "[w]hen the parents have not
entered into a shared parenting agreement." Both presumptions "rebutted **only if** the court
finds by **clear and convincing evidence** … that such an arrangement would be **detrimental**"
to the children; the objecting parent bore the burden; findings of fact and conclusions of law
required on rebuttal.

**Rebuttal factors — proposed R.C. 3109.0421** (decision-making, five factors) **and
3109.0422** (parenting time, ten factors). Notable among the latter: "(B) The geographic
proximity of the parents to each other at the time of initial filing … **and whether a parent
has relocated to impede equal parenting time**"; "(G) Whether a parent has continuously and
willfully interfered with or denied the other parent's right to parenting time in accordance
with an order of the court"; and, in both lists, "[w]hether a parent is totally incapable of
supporting or caring for the child."

**Post-rebuttal channeling — proposed R.C. 3109.0423 to 3109.0426.** After finding a presumption
rebutted, the court was required to determine whether a parent had intentionally misled the
court, made false allegations of harm to the child, or communicated false information to law
enforcement, a public children services agency, or the court "in order to gain a tactical
advantage." The majority of parenting time or decision-making then went to the parent who was
"more likely to honor and facilitate parenting time" and who had not committed those acts, and
the court was to "[c]onstruct a parenting time schedule with the child that is consistent with
ensuring the child's welfare." Proposed R.C. 3109.0424(B) and 3109.0425(B) provided that
rebutting one presumption did not rebut the other.

**Temporary orders — proposed R.C. 3109.0413(B).** Joint requests incorporated unless clear and
convincing evidence of detriment; on a contested request, "the court **shall** provide in the
temporary order equal parenting time," subject to the same clear-and-convincing detriment test,
with the burden on the objector and findings required.

**Non-marital cases — proposed R.C. 3109.12(B)(1).** "[T]here is a presumption that equal
parenting time is in the best interest of the child."

**What H.B. 14 did not do.** It **did not repeal or replace R.C. 3109.042(A)**. Its amendment to
that section changed only cross-references and the verb. An unmarried mother remained "the sole
residential parent and legal custodian of the child until a court of competent jurisdiction
issues an order." The legal-status gap survived the bill entirely.

### Opponent testimony

**Ohio State Bar Association** — Nicole Rutter-Hirth, House Families and Aging Committee,
March 21, 2023. Six objections, quoted at length in Document 1 § C.6. In summary:

1. One-size-fits-all; "overshadows the best interest of the child standard used by courts for
   decades."
2. Undermines the judiciary; "[m]andating judicial action removes discretion from the elected
   officials."
3. **Will increase litigation and cost, because of the clear-and-convincing standard** — with
   dollar figures for evaluations, drug and alcohol testing, and guardians ad litem.
4. Disadvantages low-income litigants, stay-at-home parents, and domestic-violence victims, who
   "will [be] force[d] … to prove they were abused by clear and convincing evidence."
5. **The floodgate** — "It will encourage all parents with existing custody orders that are not
   equal to come back to court … There will be a financial incentive to litigate."
6. Framing — "It requires proof that shared parenting is detrimental to the child instead of
   proof that the arrangement is in the best interest of the child."

She also stated, unprompted and twice, that she was "not opposed to equal rights when
appropriate" and "not opposed to equal parenting schedules," noting she had "filed more shared
parenting plans in the last three years than … in my first 14 years of practice" and practised in
counties offering week on/week off as a default option.

**Ohio Domestic Violence Network** — Bridget Mahoney and Maria York, same hearing; ODVN executive
director Mary O'Doherty on the amended version in 2024. Reported positions: the bill shifted Ohio
"from a child-centric model to a parent-entitlement model"; "The bill starts all families at the
finish line"; and "The bill will create a chilling effect on victims, who will be afraid to
present evidence that the other parent is unsafe."

**Others in opposition:** Legal Aid Society of Cleveland; YWCA of Dayton.

### Proponent testimony

Rep. Rodney Creech, sponsor — argued the bill reduces parental conflict: "Now that I have my
children 50% of the time, we don't fight over time any more." National Parents Organization.
Individual parents, including Anthony Slosser: "I watched my child's college fund become my
attorney's child's college fund; my retirement become my attorney's retirement."

### What the November 2023 amendment proves

The committee that heard the objections responded by **lowering the standard from clear and
convincing to preponderance** and by softening "equal" to "substantially equal." Those are
precisely the two changes the Ohio State Bar Association's third and fourth objections invited.
Resistance nevertheless continued at the May 15, 2024 hearing, and the bill never reached the
floor.

The lesson is not that the amendments were pointless. It is that **by 2024 the burden-of-proof
objection was no longer the real obstacle** — the institutional objection had become the
presumption itself, and the coalition arrayed against it was broader than the burden question.
That is what makes the vehicle choice in this project decisive: the same policy attached to a
bill the institutional coalition owns is a materially different proposition from the same policy
in a bill they oppose.

### Scope

Amends 105 sections; renumbers four for the purpose of adopting new numbers (3109.043 →
3109.0497; 3109.051 → 3109.0515; 3109.054 → 3109.0550; 3109.055 → 3109.0570); enacts new
sections 3109.041, 3109.042, 3109.054 and 3109.055 plus approximately 145 further sections in the
3109.044–3109.0529 range and R.C. 3119.071 and 3119.072; and repeals R.C. 3109.041, 3109.042 and
3109.053.

### Substance

Set out in detail in Document 1 § C.2 and compared provision-by-provision in Document 5. The
headline moves: replaces "residential parent and legal custodian" with "designated parent and
legal custodian"; **repeals the concepts of shared parenting and sole custody**; routes every case
through a parenting plan; establishes 26 best-interest factors; establishes a restrictions regime
on a preponderance standard; requires a 28-day hearing on a motion alleging interference with
parenting time; establishes a full relocation regime; removes the residential/non-residential
distinction from records-access law; and preserves pre-effective-date decrees.

**On equal parenting time specifically:** S.B. 174 requires a parenting plan to "maximize
parenting time with each parent when it is in the best interest of the child" (proposed
R.C. 3109.044) and permits a court to deny a request for substantially equal parenting time if it
"is not in the best interest of the child, endangers the safety of the parties, or for other good
cause shown," with written findings (proposed R.C. 3109.046, 3109.047). **It creates no
presumption.** The As Passed by the Senate version also provides that "[t]he court shall have
complete discretion over the approval of a parenting plan" (proposed R.C. 3109.0412).

**On unmarried parents:** proposed R.C. 3109.0424 carries forward the sole-custodian rule of
current R.C. 3109.042(A) essentially verbatim.

### The pending House substitute (l_136_0493-6)

Tabulated in Document 1 § C.3. The changes most relevant here: "parenting responsibilities"
becomes "parental rights and parenting responsibilities"; the R.C. 3109.044 purpose clause is
flipped from "when it is in the best interest" to "unless it is not in the best interest"; courts
must give "special weight" to joint parenting plans and to agreed provisions of separate plans;
joint plans must be approved **unless** found not in the child's best interest; **proposed
R.C. 3109.0412 is deleted**; a "least restrictive means" requirement is added to proposed
R.C. 3109.0455; and **compensatory parenting time becomes mandatory** on a finding of unreasonable
interference under proposed R.C. 3109.0493.

The substitute does **not** add a presumption and does **not** touch proposed R.C. 3109.0424.

## PART 4 — WHAT THE THREE HISTORIES TOGETHER ESTABLISH

**1. The coalition inverted between the 135th and 136th General Assemblies.**
H.B. 508 and H.B. 14 were sponsor-driven equal-parenting bills opposed by the Ohio State Bar
Association, the Ohio Domestic Violence Network, and legal aid organizations; both died in
committee. S.B. 174 is an institution-driven procedural rewrite supported by all of those
organizations plus the Ohio Judicial Conference and three judges' associations, and opposed by the
leading shared-parenting organization. It passed the Senate on its first attempt.

**2. The burden-of-proof fight is settled, and the presumption fight is not.**
H.B. 14's own committee conceded the burden question in November 2023 by moving to preponderance.
No enacted state presumption except Arkansas uses clear and convincing. A bill using preponderance
forfeits nothing and disarms the most quantified objection in the Ohio record.

**3. Both sides of the current fight have publicly asked for something this proposal contains.**
National Parents Organization asked for a rebuttable presumption, for codification of the
fit-parent presumption, and for outcome metrics. The Ohio State Bar Association's own S.B. 174
messaging asserts that "[e]qual parenting time is preferred" and that requiring written reasons
"reduces and limits judicial discretion." The gap between "preferred" and "presumed" is one
sentence — § 3109.086(A) — and the supporters' own framing makes it difficult to argue that
writing that sentence changes the bill's character.

**4. Three things no Ohio bill in any of the three General Assemblies has addressed.**
- The rule that an unmarried mother is sole legal custodian by operation of law. H.B. 508, H.B. 14,
  S.B. 174 as passed, and the pending House substitute all leave it standing.
- Any statewide minimum quantum of parenting time. H.B. 508 came closest, by requiring conforming
  local rules.
- Any statutory definition of, or penalty for, interference with a parent-child relationship
  beyond best-interest factors and contempt. No Ohio bill in this line has contained a criminal
  provision.

**5. The most useful single sentence in the entire record, for drafting purposes,** is from the
Ohio State Bar Association's opposition to H.B. 14: "every family court, both domestic and
juvenile, have what is called a standard or model parenting order included in their local rules …
many of those courts have expanded their standard orders and now have multiple default parenting
schedules available, several of which include schedules of equal time." Read with National Parents
Organization's count of 45 counties still using every-other-weekend defaults, it establishes both
that the infrastructure for a statewide floor already exists and that its content varies enough to
justify one.
