## PART A — CURRENT OHIO LAW

### A.1 R.C. 3109.04 — Allocation of parental rights and responsibilities **[CURRENT LAW]**

Effective in relevant part since April 11, 1991. The operative architecture:

- **Division (A)** gives the court two, and only two, ways to allocate: (1) allocate
  "primarily to one of the parents," designate that parent "the residential parent and
  the legal custodian," and divide the remaining rights — or (2) if a parent has filed
  both a pleading/motion under division (G) *and* a shared parenting plan, and the plan
  is in the child's best interest, issue a shared parenting order.
- **Consequence:** shared parenting in Ohio is *opt-in and pleading-dependent*. A parent
  who does not file a plan under division (G) cannot receive shared parenting. There is
  no default toward equality; the default is sole residential parent.
- **Division (B)** requires the best-interest determination and governs in-chambers
  interviews of the child. The court "in its discretion may and, upon the request of
  either party, shall interview in chambers." Division (B)(2)(b) requires the court to
  determine the child's reasoning ability first.
- **Division (F)(1)** lists the best-interest factors.
- **Division (E)(1)(a)** is the modification standard and is quoted in full because it
  controls the transition question (Part XXIII of the project brief):

> "The court shall not modify a prior decree allocating parental rights and
> responsibilities for the care of children unless it finds, based on facts that have
> arisen since the prior decree or that were unknown to the court at the time of the
> prior decree, that a change has occurred in the circumstances of the child, the
> child's residential parent, or either of the parents subject to a shared parenting
> decree, and that the modification is necessary to serve the best interest of the
> child. In applying these standards, the court shall retain the residential parent
> designated by the prior decree or the prior shared parenting decree, unless a
> modification is in the best interest of the child and one of the following applies:
> (i) [parental agreement]; (ii) [integration into the family of the person seeking to
> become residential parent]; (iii) The harm likely to be caused by a change of
> environment is outweighed by the advantages of the change of environment to the child."

**Drafting significance.** Any change to the *initial* allocation standard is inert for
existing orders unless the legislature also touches (E)(1)(a). Leaving (E)(1)(a) alone
is what prevents retroactive reopening.

### A.2 R.C. 3109.042 — Child born to an unmarried mother **[CURRENT LAW]**

Effective March 23, 2015. Quoted in full:

> "(A) An unmarried female who gives birth to a child is the sole residential parent and
> legal custodian of the child until a court of competent jurisdiction issues an order
> designating another person as the residential parent and legal custodian. A court
> designating the residential parent and legal custodian of a child described in this
> section shall treat the mother and father as standing upon an equality when making the
> designation.
>
> (B) Notwithstanding division (A) of this section, an unmarried female who has been
> convicted of or pleaded guilty to rape or sexual battery and has been declared under
> section 3109.501 of the Revised Code to be the parent of a child born as a result of
> rape or sexual battery shall not be a residential parent and legal custodian of that
> child."

**The structural problem this creates.** Establishing parentage under Chapter 3111 or by
acknowledgment under R.C. 3111.20–3111.35 makes a man a legal *parent* with a support
obligation. It does **not** give him any parental status as against R.C. 3109.042(A). To
obtain ordinary parenting status he must affirmatively file — under R.C. 3109.12 he "may
file a complaint requesting that the court … grant him reasonable parenting time rights."
The statute's own verb is "grant," not "recognize." Until he sues and prevails, the
mother is sole residential parent and legal custodian by operation of law. A married
father in an identical factual position begins at parity under R.C. 3109.04.

Note the internal tension: division (A)'s second sentence commands equality "when making
the designation," while its first sentence has already assigned sole custody to one
parent by statute pending that designation. The equality command operates only after
litigation the statute itself makes necessary.

### A.3 R.C. 3109.12 — Parenting time for the father of a child born to an unmarried woman **[CURRENT LAW]**

Effective March 22, 2001. Division (A) lets the father "file a complaint requesting that
the court of appropriate jurisdiction of the county in which the child resides grant him
reasonable parenting time rights with the child" once paternity is acknowledged and final
or adjudicated under Chapter 3111. Division (B): the court "may grant" those rights "if
it determines that the granting of the parenting time rights … is in the best interest of
the child," considering the R.C. 3109.051(D) factors.

In **Harrold v. Collier**, 107 Ohio St.3d 44, 2005-Ohio-5334, the Supreme Court of Ohio
upheld R.C. 3109.11 and 3109.12 against a substantive-due-process challenge under
*Troxel v. Granville*, 530 U.S. 57 (2000), holding that the *Troxel* presumption that a
fit parent acts in the child's best interest is rebuttable and that Ohio courts must give
"special weight" to a fit parent's wishes.

### A.4 R.C. 3109.051 — Parenting time **[CURRENT LAW]**

The provisions that matter for this project:

- **(A)** Where there is no shared parenting decree, the court "shall make a just and
  reasonable order or decree permitting each parent who is not the residential parent to
  have parenting time … unless the court determines that it would not be in the best
  interest of the child." It adds: "Whenever possible, the order or decree permitting the
  parenting time shall ensure the opportunity for both parents to have frequent and
  continuing contact with the child." The court "shall include in its final decree a
  specific schedule of parenting time for that parent."
- **(D)** Eighteen factors, including **(D)(10)**, "Each parent's willingness to
  reschedule missed parenting time and to facilitate the other parent's parenting time
  rights."
- **(F)(1)** If the court *denies* parenting time and the parent files a written request,
  the court "shall state in writing its findings of fact and conclusions of law in
  accordance with Civil Rule 52." Findings are thus currently required only on outright
  denial, and only on request.
- **(F)(2)** — **the statutory hook for a minimum schedule**, quoted in full:

> "On or before July 1, 1991, each court of common pleas, by rule, shall adopt standard
> parenting time guidelines. A court shall have discretion to deviate from its standard
> parenting time guidelines based upon factors set forth in division (D) of this section."

  Ohio therefore already has a statewide *mandate* that every common pleas court publish
  a default schedule, but no statewide *content*. The mandate dates from the 1990-91
  shared-parenting reforms that produced R.C. 3109.051 in its current form. Eighty-eight counties write eighty-eight
  schedules, and the statute expressly authorizes deviation from them. Converting
  (F)(2) from a delegation into a floor is a one-subsection change, not a new regime.
- **(G)** Relocation notice by the residential parent.
- **(H)–(J)** Records and school access; **(K)** contempt.

**(K)** is the existing enforcement provision and is quoted because the proposal builds on
it rather than replacing it:

> "If any person is found in contempt of court for failing to comply with or interfering
> with any order or decree granting parenting time rights … the court that makes the
> finding, in addition to any other penalty or remedy imposed, **shall** assess all court
> costs arising out of the contempt proceeding against the person and require the person
> to pay any reasonable attorney's fees of any adverse party … and **may** award
> reasonable compensatory parenting time or visitation … if such compensatory parenting
> time or visitation is in the best interest of the child."

**Note the asymmetry.** Costs and fees are already mandatory on a contempt finding.
Make-up time — the remedy the wronged parent and the child actually need — is
discretionary. That asymmetry is the single most consequential defect in Ohio's
enforcement law and is the easiest to fix.

### A.5 R.C. 2919.23 — Interference with custody **[CURRENT LAW]**

Effective April 6, 2023 (H.B. 281, 134th G.A.). Quoted in full:

> "(A) No person, knowing the person is without privilege to do so or being reckless in
> that regard, shall entice, take, keep, or harbor a person identified in division (A)(1),
> (2), or (3) of this section from the parent, guardian, or custodian of the person
> identified in division (A)(1), (2), or (3) of this section: (1) A child under the age of
> eighteen, or a child with a mental or physical disability under the age of twenty-one;
> (2) A person committed by law to an institution for delinquent, unruly, neglected,
> abused, or dependent children; (3) A person committed by law to an institution for
> persons with mental illnesses or an institution for persons with intellectual
> disabilities.
>
> (B) No person shall aid, abet, induce, cause, or encourage a child or a ward of the
> juvenile court who has been committed to the custody of any person, department, or
> public or private institution to leave the custody of that person, department, or
> institution without legal consent.
>
> (C) It is an affirmative defense to a charge of enticing or taking under division (A)(1)
> of this section, that the actor reasonably believed that the actor's conduct was
> necessary to preserve the child's health or safety. It is an affirmative defense to a
> charge of keeping or harboring under division (A) of this section, that the actor in
> good faith gave notice to law enforcement or judicial authorities within a reasonable
> time after the child or committed person came under the actor's shelter, protection, or
> influence.
>
> (D)(1) Whoever violates this section is guilty of interference with custody. (2) Except
> as otherwise provided in this division, a violation of division (A)(1) of this section
> is a misdemeanor of the first degree. If the child who is the subject of a violation of
> division (A)(1) of this section is removed from the state or if the offender previously
> has been convicted of an offense under this section, a violation of division (A)(1) of
> this section is a felony of the fifth degree. If the child who is the subject of a
> violation of division (A)(1) of this section suffers physical harm as a result of the
> violation, a violation of division (A)(1) of this section is a felony of the fourth
> degree. (3) A violation of division (A)(2) or (3) of this section is a misdemeanor of
> the third degree. (4) A violation of division (B) of this section is a misdemeanor of
> the first degree. Each day of violation of division (B) of this section is a separate
> offense."

### A.6 *Toledo v. Parra* and the "it's just a civil matter" problem

**Toledo v. Parra**, 6th Dist. Lucas No. L-12-1261, **2013-Ohio-3182** (July 19, 2013).
The mother was sole residential parent and legal custodian by domestic relations order;
the father had court-ordered parenting time. He filed a criminal complaint under Toledo
Municipal Code 515.04, which the court found "identical to R.C. 2919.23" except for
"minor language modernization." She was convicted on a no-contest plea and fined $50.

Three holdings matter.

1. **A custodial parent can commit the offense.** The court rejected the argument that
   the offense title limits it to non-custodians, citing R.C. 1.01 (headings are not part
   of the law): "appellant's argument that the title of the ordinance/statute excludes
   applicability to a legal custodian is not persuasive." (¶ 9.)
2. **Withholding ordered parenting time supplies the "without privilege" element.** ¶ 12:
   "If … appellant withheld visitation with the minor children from the complainant
   during the time such visitation was ordered by the court, this constitutes acting with
   knowledge that she was without privilege to keep the children from the parent who, by
   court order, was entitled to their custody at that time. **This may be criminally
   prosecuted.**"
3. **Domestic relations contempt is not the exclusive remedy.** The mother argued the
   proper forum was contempt under R.C. 2705.031. ¶ 17: "While we may find understandable
   appellant's position that custody and visitation matters are best addressed in a
   domestic relations setting, **appellant has directed our attention to no authority that
   mandates deference to such court.**" ¶ 18 adds that "[t]he sometimes complicated
   evidentiary issues in proving an offense in which the element of knowledge of
   privilege, or absence thereof, is based on the interpretation of the order of another
   court **may implicate prosecutorial discretion**. There is nothing in the law, however,
   that dictates dismissal of a criminal offense properly before the court."

Earlier consistent authority the court relied on: **State v. Sprinkle**, 12th Dist. No.
CA2006-08-101, 2007-Ohio-4967, ¶ 42 (mother failed to return child in violation of shared
parenting agreement); **State v. Brickles**, 2d Dist. No. 17526, 1999 WL 957643 (Sept. 3,
1999) (failure to return daughter after visitation); **State v. Skelly**, 2d Dist. No.
13306, 1992 WL 361833 (Dec. 7, 1992). Compare **City of Toledo v. Jenkins**, 6th Dist.
No. L-12-1223, 2013-Ohio-3058 (pleading sufficiency of an identically worded complaint).

**What this means for drafting.** The authority to prosecute already exists. What does
not exist is (a) a statute whose elements are *written for* parenting-time denial rather
than for child-stealing, and (b) any statutory answer to the practical reality that ¶ 18
identifies — prosecutors decline these cases because the elements do not fit and because
they read the dispute as civil. A codified non-exclusivity clause plus purpose-built
elements addresses both. It does not create new authority; it makes existing authority
usable.

### A.7 Ohio's "Rule 22" schedule — identification and content

The project brief refers to "the Ohio Rule 22 parenting schedule." The exact rule is:

**Franklin County Court of Common Pleas, Division of Domestic Relations and Juvenile
Branch — Local Domestic Court Rule 27 / Local Juvenile Court Rule 22**, and the schedule
itself is the Model Parenting Time Schedule attached as **Local Domestic Rule 27.1 /
Local Juvenile Rule 22.1** (effective January 1, 2015 and thereafter). This is the rule
adopted under the mandate of R.C. 3109.051(F)(2). It is a *county* rule, not a statewide
one, and it is expressly "merely a guideline for parenting time," with tailoring "the
parties' responsibility."

Structure of the Franklin County model schedule:

- **Distance tiers**: separate schedules for parents under 90 miles apart one way and
  over 90 miles one way.
- **Under 90 miles — four options:**
  - **Option A** — equal alternating weeks, Sunday 6:00 p.m. transitions, plus a midweek
    Wednesday evening 5:00–8:00 p.m. *(This is a 50/50 schedule already published as a
    county default option.)*
  - **Option B** — alternating weekends Friday 6:00 p.m.–Monday 6:00 p.m. plus split
    weekdays.
  - **Option C** — alternating weekends Friday 6:00 p.m.–Monday 6:00 p.m. plus one
    weekday overnight (Wednesday).
  - **Option D** — alternating weekends Friday 6:00 p.m.–Sunday 6:00 p.m. plus one
    weekday evening Wednesday 5:00–8:00 p.m.
- **Holidays**: odd/even-year rotation of MLK Day, spring break, Independence Day,
  Thanksgiving, winter break.
- **Summer**: two-week alternating blocks with the weekend rotation continuing.
- **Age tiers**: pre-school children follow the school-age schedule; infants from birth
  to 18 months receive shorter, more frequent time (alternating 24-hour weekend periods,
  Tuesday/Thursday 5:00–8:00 p.m.).
- **Communication**: children may contact either parent "as often as they wish, at
  reasonable times," with a minimum of three contacts weekly.

**Can it be converted into a statewide statutory minimum? Yes, and there is a tested
template for exactly that.** Utah has done it — see Part B.5. Option D is the natural
floor candidate: it is the least generous of the four Franklin County options, it is
recognizable to every Ohio practitioner, and it maps almost exactly onto the Utah
statutory minimum (one weekday evening + alternating weekends + holiday rotation +
extended summer time). Drafting the floor as Option-D-equivalent means the floor is
*below* what many counties already give, which is the point: a floor should be
uncontroversial.

**Important caution against overstating the floor's novelty.** The Ohio State Bar
Association's own H.B. 14 testimony confirms the county-rule landscape has already moved:
"every family court, both domestic and juvenile, have what is called a standard or model
parenting order included in their local rules. … In the past five years, many of those
courts have expanded their standard orders and now have multiple default parenting
schedules available, several of which include schedules of equal time." Against that,
National Parents Organization told the House Judiciary Committee on June 3, 2026 that "45
Ohio counties still rely on archaic 'every other weekend' default schedules" and "30 Ohio
counties still use the outdated, offensive language" of visitation. Both statements can be
true; they describe different halves of an uneven map. That unevenness is the case for a
statewide floor.

## PART B — COMPARATIVE STATE LAW (ENACTED STATUTES ONLY)

### B.1 West Virginia — the simplicity model

**W.Va. Code § 48-9-102a**, enacted by the Best Interests of Child Protection Act of
2022, S.B. 463. The entire section, verbatim, is two sentences:

> "There shall be a presumption, rebuttable by a preponderance of the evidence, that
> equal (50-50) custodial allocation is in the best interest of the child. If the
> presumption is rebutted, the court shall, absent an agreement between the parents as to
> all matters related to custodial allocation, construct a parenting time schedule which
> maximizes the time each parent has with the child and is consistent with ensuring the
> child's welfare."

Four design lessons, all adopted in this proposal:

1. **Preponderance**, not clear and convincing.
2. **One rule, one sentence.** No factor list attached to the presumption itself; the
   existing best-interest architecture in § 48-9-102 and § 48-9-209 does the work.
3. **A mandatory fallback.** Rebuttal does not return the court to an open field; it
   routes the court to the maximize-time schedule. This is the provision that prevents
   "50/50 rejected → every other weekend."
4. **An agreement carve-out.** Parental agreement displaces the presumption entirely.

West Virginia did *not* enact a separate statutory minimum floor, a separate criminal
interference offense, or an express temporary-order presumption in § 48-9-102a itself.
Those gaps are filled in this proposal from other jurisdictions. Do not represent
West Virginia as having them.

The West Virginia judiciary published practitioner guidance on the new section — *The New
"50/50" Law: Rebutting the Presumption in Theory and Practice* (courtswv.gov, Sept. 23,
2022) — which is useful for anticipating how Ohio judges would ask about implementation.

### B.2 Kentucky — the oldest presumption, and its 2026 recalibration

**KRS 403.270(2)** **[CURRENT LAW, as amended effective July 15, 2026]**:

> "The court shall determine custody in accordance with the best interests of the child
> and equal consideration shall be given to each parent and to any de facto custodian.
> Subject to KRS 403.315, there shall be a presumption, rebuttable by a preponderance of
> evidence, that joint custody and equally shared parenting time is in the best interests
> of the child. If a deviation from equal parenting time is warranted, the court shall
> construct a parenting time schedule which maximizes the time each parent or de facto
> custodian has with the child and is consistent with ensuring the child's welfare."

The presumption dates from 2018 Ky. Acts ch. 198, § 1 (eff. July 14, 2018). Kentucky was
the first state to enact a permanent joint-custody-and-equal-time presumption.

**The 2026 amendment is the most important recent development in the field and should
shape this bill.** 2026 Ky. Acts ch. 92 (eff. July 15, 2026) restructured how domestic
violence interacts with the presumption:

- New **KRS 403.270(3)** requires the court, **before** reaching any other best-interest
  factor, to "(a) Consider any allegations of: 1. Domestic violence and abuse as defined
  in KRS 403.720; or 2. An abused or neglected child as defined in KRS 600.020; committed
  by one (1) party against another party or a child of the parties; and (b) Make written
  findings on the record as to the impact, if any, of the allegations described in
  paragraph (a) of this subsection on the child."
- **KRS 403.270(4)(h)** keeps as a factor "A finding by the court that a party has
  maliciously made an intentionally false allegation of child abuse which has no basis in
  fact and which the party knew had no basis in fact against another party." Note the
  triple guard: *maliciously* + *intentionally false* + *knew had no basis in fact*. This
  is the drafting model for Part XVIII.
- **KRS 403.270(4)(l)** makes "[t]he likelihood a party will allow the child frequent,
  meaningful, and continuing contact with the other parent" a factor, **but expressly
  forbids the court from considering it** against a party where the *other* parent
  committed domestic violence against that party or a child and continuing contact would
  endanger health or safety. This is an elegant anti-weaponization clause: it prevents a
  "friendly parent" factor from being turned against an abuse victim who resists contact.
- **KRS 403.315** **[CURRENT LAW, as amended eff. July 15, 2026]**, verbatim:

> "(1) When determining or modifying a custody order pursuant to KRS 403.270, 403.280,
> 403.340, or 403.740, the court shall consider the safety and well-being of the parties
> and of the children. If a domestic violence order is being or has been entered against
> a party by another party or on behalf of a child at issue in the custody hearing, the
> presumption that joint custody and equally shared parenting time is in the best
> interests of the child shall not apply as to the party against whom the domestic
> violence order is being or has been entered. The court shall weigh all factors set out
> in KRS 403.270 in determining the best interests of the child.
> (2) There shall be a presumption, rebuttable by a preponderance of the evidence, that
> joint custody and equally shared parenting time is not in the best interests of the
> child if the court finds that a party has committed two (2) or more acts of domestic
> violence and abuse as defined in KRS 403.270 against another party.
> (3)(a) The court shall not make an award of custody or grant unsupervised visitation
> with the child to a party who is found to have committed two (2) or more acts of
> domestic violence and abuse as defined in KRS 403.720 against another party unless that
> party has: 1. Completed: a. A batterer's intervention program provided by a certified
> provider …; b. Parenting classes; and c. Substance use and mental health assessments and
> any recommended corresponding treatments; and 2. Participated in supervised visitation.
> (b) Following a party's completion of the requirements in paragraph (a) of this
> subsection, the court shall conduct an evidentiary hearing before ordering unsupervised
> visitation to that party and a second or subsequent hearing before awarding custodial
> rights to that party."

**Why this matters politically in Ohio.** The Kentucky model shows a legislature keeping
a 50/50 presumption for eight years and then *strengthening the domestic-violence
architecture around it* rather than abandoning it. Ohio's most effective opponents of
H.B. 14 were domestic-violence organizations. A bill that pairs the presumption with a
Kentucky-style front-loaded DV screen, a DVO switch-off, and a two-acts reverse
presumption is answering that objection with an enacted model rather than with
reassurance.

One caution: 2026 Ky. Acts ch. 92 introduced a citation error. The Legislative Research
Commission's own note records that KRS 403.315(3)(a)1.a. cites "920 KAR 5:020," "though
no such administrative regulation exists. 922 KAR 5:020 appears to be on point, but it is
not manifestly clear from the text of the Act." Do not replicate the cross-reference
style that produced it.

### B.3 Arkansas

**Ark. Code Ann. § 9-13-101** **[CURRENT LAW]** (presumption added 2021):

- "Award of custody of a child of the marriage shall be made without regard to the sex of
  a parent but solely in accordance with the welfare and best interest of the child."
- "In an action concerning an original child custody determination in a divorce or
  paternity matter, there is a rebuttable presumption that joint custody is in the best
  interest of the child."
- **Definition, which is the operative move:** "'Joint custody' means the approximate and
  reasonable equal division of time with the child by both parents individually as agreed
  to by the parties or as ordered by the court."
- Rebuttal is by **clear and convincing** evidence that joint custody is not in the
  child's best interest — *or* the presumption simply does not apply where the parties
  have agreed on all custody issues, or where no party requests sole, primary, or joint
  custody, or where a subsection (c) or (d) presumption is established.
- **Counter-presumptions**: "There is a rebuttable presumption that it is not in the best
  interest of the child to be placed in the custody of an abusive parent" where domestic
  abuse is established; and "There is a rebuttable presumption that it is not in the best
  interest of the child to be placed in the care or custody of a sex offender or to have
  unsupervised visitation."

**Two lessons.** First, Arkansas achieves "50/50" by *defining* joint custody as
approximately equal time rather than by writing "equal parenting time" in the presumption
— a drafting route worth knowing but not adopted here, because it forces a definitional
fight and Ohio's S.B. 174 vocabulary already separates parenting time from
decision-making. Second, Arkansas's "presumption does not apply where the parties have
agreed" and "does not apply where no party requests joint custody" exclusions are
sensible litigation-avoidance devices and are adopted here in modified form.

Note that Arkansas is the outlier on burden: it uses clear and convincing. It is the only
one of the four enacted equal-time presumptions to do so.

### B.4 Florida — the newest and most Ohio-relevant model

**Fla. Stat. § 61.13(2)(c)** **[CURRENT LAW]**, amended by 2023 Fla. Laws ch. 2023-213
(H.B. 1301), effective July 1, 2023:

> "there is a rebuttable presumption that equal time-sharing of a minor child is in the
> best interests of the minor child" — rebutted where "a party must prove by a
> preponderance of the evidence that equal time-sharing is not in the best interests of
> the minor child."

And on findings:

> "Except when a time-sharing schedule is agreed to by the parties and approved by the
> court, the court must evaluate all of the factors set forth in subsection (3) and make
> specific written findings of fact when creating or modifying a time-sharing schedule."

This replaced prior language stating there was "no presumption for or against the father
or mother … or for or against any specific time-sharing schedule." The presumption
reaches chapter 61 dissolution cases, chapter 742 paternity cases (which incorporate
§ 61.13 by reference), and modification proceedings. Florida courts and commentators have
litigated whether the presumption applies retroactively to pre-2023 orders — see *The
Florida Bar Journal*, "Retroactivity (or Not) of the Equal Timesharing Presumption."

**Lesson.** Florida is the cleanest precedent for the exact combination this project
wants: rebuttable presumption + preponderance + mandatory written findings + coverage of
non-marital cases through incorporation by reference. It is also a warning: Florida's
failure to state the transition rule explicitly generated appellate litigation. **State
the transition rule.**

### B.5 Utah — the statutory minimum schedule (the model for the floor)

**Utah Code § 30-3-35**, "Minimum schedule for parent-time for a child five to 18 years
old" (eff. May 3, 2023; renumbered Sept. 1, 2024). Verbatim in relevant part:

> "(2) The parent-time schedule in this section applies to a child who is five to 18
> years old.
> (3) If the parties do not agree to a parent-time schedule for a child described in
> Subsection (2), the following schedule is considered the minimum parent-time to which
> the noncustodial parent is entitled to the child:
> (a)(i) one weekday evening to be specified by the noncustodial parent or the court or
> Wednesday evening if not specified, beginning at 5:30 p.m. and ending at 8:30 p.m.; or
> (ii) at the election of the noncustodial parent, one weekday to be specified by the
> noncustodial parent or the court: (A) beginning at the time that the child's school is
> regularly dismissed and ending at 8:30 p.m.; or (B) if school is not in session, the
> noncustodial parent is available to be with the child, and in accommodation with the
> custodial parent's work schedule, beginning at 9 a.m. and ending at 8:30 p.m.;
> (b)(i) beginning on the first weekend after entry of the decree, alternating weekends
> beginning at 6 p.m. on Friday and ending on Sunday at 7 p.m.; or (ii) at the election of
> the noncustodial parent … alternating weekends: (A) beginning at the time that the
> child's school is regularly dismissed on Friday and ending on Sunday at 7 p.m.; or
> (B) if school is not in session … beginning on Friday at 9 a.m. and ending on Sunday at
> 7 p.m.;
> (c) each holiday granted to the noncustodial parent in accordance with the holiday
> schedule described in Subsection (13); and
> (d) extended parent-time with the child when school is not in session for summer break
> in accordance with Subsection (4)."

Subsection (4)(a): "the noncustodial parent is entitled up to four weeks of parent-time
with the child, which may be consecutive, when school is not in session for summer
break," of which two weeks "shall be uninterrupted."

Related Utah sections: **§ 30-3-35.5** (minimum schedule for children under five);
**§ 30-3-35.1** (optional increased schedule); **§ 30-3-35.2** (equal parent-time
schedule, available on stated conditions).

**This is the single most important comparative find for Part IX.** Utah proves that a
legislature can put a concrete, hour-specific default schedule into a statute; that the
schedule survives in practice; and that the governing trigger is simply "[i]f the parties
do not agree." Compare it to the Franklin County Option D schedule described at A.7: one
weekday evening, alternating weekends, holiday rotation, extended summer time. They are
the same instrument. A statewide Ohio floor is a transplant, not an invention.

### B.6 Minnesota — the percentage floor and graduated enforcement

**Minn. Stat. § 518.175, subd. 1** **[CURRENT LAW]**: "In the absence of other evidence,
there is a rebuttable presumption that a child must receive a minimum of at least 25
percent of the parenting time with each parent." The statute permits the percentage to be
computed by overnights "or by using a method other than overnights if the court can find
a reliable method for calculating equivalent parenting time."

**Subd. 6** (remedies) requires the court to "fully consider providing compensatory
parenting time when a parent has intentionally made a substantial amount of court-ordered
parenting time unavailable to the other parent"; and where a parent "repeatedly and
intentionally denied or interfered with court-ordered parenting time," the court **shall**
award compensatory parenting time and **may** impose a civil penalty up to $500, modify
custody, require reimbursement of costs and attorney fees, or award other relief.
Unwarranted denial may be treated as contempt.

**Lessons.** (1) Minnesota shows a *percentage* floor is drafted and administered without
difficulty — but note it is 25%, a genuine floor, not a target, which is exactly the
architecture the project brief wants. (2) The "repeatedly and intentionally" trigger for
mandatory make-up time plus discretionary escalation is the civil-remedy ladder this
proposal adopts.

**Minn. Stat. § 609.26** (Depriving another of custodial or parental rights) supplies the
best-drafted **affirmative-defense set** in the country for Part XVII. It is an
affirmative defense if the person proves:

1. "the person reasonably believed the action taken was necessary to protect the child
   from physical or sexual assault or substantial emotional harm";
2. "the person reasonably believed the action taken was necessary to protect the person
   taking the action from physical or sexual assault";
3. "the action taken is consented to by the parent, stepparent, or legal custodian
   seeking prosecution, **but consent to custody or specific parenting time is not
   consent to the action of failing to return or concealing a minor child**"; or
4. "the action taken is otherwise authorized by a court order issued prior to the
   violation."

And the statute adds: "The defenses provided in this subdivision are in addition to and
do not limit other defenses available under this chapter or chapter 611." Minnesota also
mandates dismissal of a felony charge where the person voluntarily returns the child
within 48 hours, or where the person and child never left the state and within seven days
a family-court proceeding is commenced.

The italicised clause in defense (3) is a subtle and necessary piece of drafting: without
it, a parent could claim that the other parent's general consent to the schedule
immunised a particular concealment.

### B.7 Illinois — the two-track model (civil expedited + minor criminal offense)

**750 ILCS 5/607.5** ("Abuse of allocated parenting time") **[CURRENT LAW]**: "The court
shall provide an expedited procedure for the enforcement of allocated parenting time."
The petition must state the parties, the current parenting-plan terms, the specific
violations, and evidence of attempted dispute resolution. On a finding of non-compliance
the court may impose one or more of: additional terms consistent with prior orders;
mandatory parental education at the non-complying parent's expense; family or individual
counseling with costs allocated; a cash bond or security with forfeiture; "[m]akeup
parenting time … of the same type and duration … made up within 6 months"; a contempt
finding; civil fines per denied incident; reimbursement of reasonable expenses; and other
best-interest provisions. On a contempt finding coupled with parenting-time abuse the
court may suspend driving privileges, impose probation, impose periodic imprisonment up
to six months, and impose petty-offense fines up to $500 per violation. Attorney's fees,
court costs and expenses **must** be ordered absent good cause.

**720 ILCS 5/10-5.5** ("Unlawful visitation or parenting time interference")
**[CURRENT LAW]**: "Every person who, in violation of the visitation, parenting time, or
custody time provisions of a court order relating to child custody, detains or conceals a
child with the intent to deprive another person of his or her rights to visitation,
parenting time, or custody time commits the offense of unlawful visitation or parenting
time interference." It is a **petty offense**; "any person violating this Section after 2
prior convictions … is guilty of a Class A misdemeanor." Affirmative defenses: reasonable
belief the act was necessary to protect the child from imminent physical harm; mutual
consent of all parties with custody or visitation rights; or conduct otherwise authorized
by law. Enforcement is by written notice to appear issued by a peace officer, with a
summons or warrant on failure to appear.

**Lessons.** (1) Illinois is the clearest working example of *both* tracks the project
brief wants, operating side by side, with no evidence that the criminal track has
swallowed the civil one — because the criminal track is deliberately minor and the civil
track is deliberately fast. (2) The "detains or conceals … with the intent to deprive"
formulation is objective, conduct-based, and has survived decades. (3) Illinois's
escalation is *conviction*-indexed (two priors → Class A misdemeanor), which is a cleaner
trigger than a count of underlying incidents.

### B.8 Arizona — the civil menu and the "maximize" directive

**A.R.S. § 25-414** ("Violation of visitation or parenting time rights; penalties")
**[CURRENT LAW]** requires the court, on a verified petition alleging violation, to take
at least one of a listed set of actions: "[f]ind the violating parent in contempt of
court"; "[o]rder visitation or parenting time to make up for the missed sessions";
"[o]rder parent education at the violating parent's expense"; "[o]rder family counseling
at the violating parent's expense"; civil penalties "not to exceed one hundred dollars for
each violation"; or order mediation or other ADR. Procedurally: "[w]ithin twenty-five days
of service of the petition the court shall hold a hearing or conference before a judge,
commissioner or person appointed by the court to review noncompliance." Court costs and
attorney fees "shall be paid by the violating parent."

**A.R.S. § 25-403.02** **[CURRENT LAW]**: "the court shall adopt a parenting plan that
provides for both parents to share legal decision-making regarding their child and that
maximizes their respective parenting time." It also provides that the court "shall not
prefer a parent's proposed plan because of the parent's or child's gender," and — an
important clarification this proposal borrows — "[s]hared legal decision-making does not
necessarily mean equal parenting time."

**Lessons.** (1) A short, mandatory menu ("the court shall take at least one of the
following") is more administrable than either pure discretion or a rigid single remedy.
(2) A 25-day outside date for the first hearing is a tested number; S.B. 174's proposed
28 days is in the same range, so Ohio should simply use 28 and stay internally
consistent. (3) Arizona proves that "maximize parenting time" can coexist with the
absence of a presumption — which is precisely S.B. 174's posture, and precisely why
S.B. 174 alone does not accomplish this project's goal.

### B.9 Nevada — an order-of-preference model, and why it is not recommended

**NRS 125C.0025** **[CURRENT LAW]** creates a *preference*, not a presumption, for joint
physical custody where "[t]he parents have agreed" or "[a] parent has demonstrated, or has
attempted to demonstrate but has had his or her efforts frustrated by the other parent, an
intent to establish a meaningful relationship with the minor child." **NRS 125C.0035**
directs the court to award physical custody "in the following order of preference unless
in a particular case the best interest of the child requires otherwise: (a) To both
parents jointly pursuant to NRS 125C.0025 or to either parent. If the court does not enter
an order awarding joint physical custody of a child after either parent has applied for
joint physical custody, the court shall state in its decision the reason for its denial of
the parent's application."

The frustrated-efforts clause in NRS 125C.0025 is worth borrowing — it is a compact
anti-manufacture provision. The *order of preference* device is not recommended: it
invites litigation about what "preference" means, and Ohio already has enough
undefined-weight problems (see the "special weight" issue at C.4).

### B.10 Texas — the enforcement model (2025)

**Tex. H.B. 3181, 89th Leg., R.S. (2025), effective September 1, 2025** — passed the
House 135–6 and the Senate 31–0. Quoted verbatim from the enrolled act because it is the
direct model for Parts XII and XIV:

> "Sec. 156.107. MODIFICATION OF ORDER ON FINDING OF CONTEMPT FOR DENIAL OF POSSESSION
> AND ACCESS. A finding by the court that a conservator is in contempt of court for the
> denial of court-ordered possession of or access to a child and has previously been found
> in contempt of court at least three times for failure to comply with the terms of an
> order providing for possession of or access to the child constitutes a material and
> substantial change of circumstances sufficient to justify modification of an existing
> court order …"

> "Sec. 157.165. PROBATION OF CONTEMPT ORDER. (a) Except as provided by Subsection (b),
> the court may place the respondent on community supervision and suspend commitment …
> (b) The court may not place the respondent on community supervision and suspend
> commitment if the court finds that the respondent: (1) is in contempt of court for the
> failure or refusal to obey an order for possession of or access to a child; and (2) has
> previously been found in contempt of court at least three times for the failure or
> refusal to obey an order for possession of or access to a child."

Section 157.167(e): "The court may not waive the requirement that the respondent pay
attorney's fees and costs if the court finds that the respondent has previously been found
in contempt of court at least three times for the denial of court-ordered possession of or
access to the child …"

Section 157.168(a) as amended: "**Unless a party shows good cause why the order should not
be rendered**, a court **shall** order additional periods of possession of or access to a
child to compensate for the denial of court-ordered possession or access." (The former
"may" was struck.) Subsection (a-2): the additional periods "must be of the same type and
duration of the possession or access that was denied," "may include weekend, holiday, and
summer possession or access," and "must occur on or before the second anniversary of the
date the court finds that court-ordered possession or access has been denied." New
subsection (d): where there have been at least three prior contempt findings for denial,
the make-up periods "must be, in total, **twice the duration** of the periods of possession
and access that were denied."

**This is the answer to the project brief's Part XIV question about bright lines.** Texas
uses a count of **three**, indexed to **contempt findings** rather than to raw incidents,
with **no time window at all**. That design choice is instructive: indexing to
adjudications rather than allegations means the trigger is self-proving from the court's
own docket, requires no re-litigation of old incidents, and cannot be inflated by a
parent's characterisation of events. It is the most administrable bright line found in
this research.

**A caution on a related Texas claim.** Secondary sources report that **Tex. S.B. 2794
(2025)** amended Tex. Penal Code § 25.03 (Interference with Child Custody) to remove a
"noncustodial-only" limitation and create escalating penalties (first and second offenses
Class C misdemeanors, third a state jail felony). **This project could not verify that
text against the enrolled bill or the codified section**; the Penal Code § 25.03 text
available on review still shows its most recent amendment as Acts 2023, 88th Leg., R.S.,
ch. 520 (H.B. 3025), eff. Sept. 1, 2023, and classifies the offense as a state jail
felony without a graduated scheme. **Do not cite S.B. 2794 in sponsor testimony without
pulling the enrolled bill.** H.B. 3181, by contrast, is verified verbatim and is the
stronger citation anyway.

the least-generous existing county-rule level.

### C.2 What S.B. 174 (As Passed by the Senate) actually does

Scale: it amends 105 sections, renumbers four (3109.043→3109.0497, 3109.051→3109.0515,
3109.054→3109.0550, 3109.055→3109.0570), enacts roughly 150 new sections including
3109.041 through 3109.0529 plus 3119.071 and 3119.072, and repeals R.C. 3109.041,
3109.042, and 3109.053.

Core architectural moves:

- **Terminology.** Replaces "residential parent and legal custodian" with "designated
  parent and legal custodian." **Repeals the concepts of shared parenting and sole
  custody.** "Designated parent and legal custodian" becomes a purely functional label —
  proposed R.C. 3109.04(B) defines it as a parent "designated for any of the purposes
  listed under divisions (B)(1) to (5) of section 3109.044," i.e. child support, school
  district, health coverage, tax dependency, and public assistance/treaty/other legal
  requirements. **This is a significant and underappreciated reform on its own: it
  decouples the label from the parenting arrangement.**
- **Everything runs through a parenting plan.** Proposed R.C. 3109.041(A): "A court shall
  allocate parenting responsibilities in any proceeding pertaining to the allocation of
  parenting responsibilities." Proposed R.C. 3109.042: "Allocation of parenting
  responsibilities shall be based on a parenting plan approved or issued by the court. The
  allocation of parenting responsibilities in a parenting plan does not abrogate parental
  rights recognized by the United States Constitution or Ohio Constitution."
- **Purpose clause — proposed R.C. 3109.044**, verbatim: "The purpose of a parenting plan
  is to allocate all parenting responsibilities to parents or legal custodians. The plan
  shall seek to ensure that parents or legal custodians share in the responsibilities of
  raising a child, enable a child to enjoy a meaningful relationship with both parents or
  legal custodians, **and maximize parenting time with each parent when it is in the best
  interest of the child**." Then thirteen mandatory plan contents, (A) through (M).
- **Filing.** Proposed R.C. 3109.045: plan due "not later than thirty days before a
  hearing," waivable for good cause; separate or joint plans permitted. Proposed
  R.C. 3109.046 (joint plans), 3109.047 (separate plans), 3109.048 (court issues its own
  if none filed).
- **The equal-parenting provision, and its limits.** Both proposed R.C. 3109.046 and
  3109.047 close with the same sentence: "If a joint parenting plan includes a provision
  for substantially equal parenting time, the court may deny the request if the court
  determines that substantially equal parenting time is not in the best interest of the
  child, endangers the safety of the parties, or **for other good cause shown** and
  provides written findings of fact to support the determination."
- **Proposed R.C. 3109.0412:** "The court shall have complete discretion over the
  approval of a parenting plan."
- **Proposed R.C. 3109.0411:** "In allocating or approving parenting responsibilities in
  a parenting plan, the court shall not draw any presumptions from a temporary parenting
  order."
- **Best interest — proposed R.C. 3109.0430(A)** lists 26 factors. Several are directly
  useful here: (9) "Any current abuse or history of child abuse, spousal abuse, domestic
  violence, or parental kidnapping"; **(10) "Whether a parent or legal custodian has
  knowingly made a false report of child abuse or neglect"**; **(12) "Whether a parent or
  legal custodian, without just cause, has repeatedly interfered with the other parent's
  or legal custodian's court-ordered access to the child"**; (13) willingness to
  facilitate parenting time; **(24)** intentional misleading of the court to cause delay,
  increase cost, or induce preference; **(25)** unjustified failure to comply with a
  temporary or final plan. Division (B): no preference based on financial circumstances or
  gender. Division (C): no single factor to the exclusion of others.
- **Restrictions — proposed R.C. 3109.0455:** "the court may approve or order a
  restriction of parenting responsibilities if the court finds, **based upon a
  preponderance of the evidence**, that such restrictions are reasonably calculated to
  protect a child from physical, sexual, or emotional abuse, or a parent from domestic
  violence." Proposed R.C. 3109.0456 lists ten grounds (A)–(J), including "(F) Denial of
  access of the other parent or legal custodian to the child for protracted periods of
  time without justifiable cause." Proposed R.C. 3109.0457 lists eleven available
  restrictions (A)–(K), from sole decision-making through supervised time, bonds, and "(J)
  Prohibiting all contact or parenting time." Proposed R.C. 3109.0458 requires "specific
  written findings of fact" before issuing a plan **without** restrictions where there is
  a qualifying conviction or abuse/neglect history. Proposed R.C. 3109.0459 governs
  supervisors.
- **Temporary orders.** Proposed R.C. 3109.0422 (ex parte on affidavit, for good cause,
  no oral hearing) and 3109.0423 (party may request an oral hearing; court "shall conduct
  an oral hearing not later than twenty-eight days after the filing of the request unless
  good cause is shown").
- **Unmarried parents — the critical gap.** Proposed **R.C. 3109.0424**, verbatim: "An
  unmarried female who gives birth to a child is the sole designated parent and legal
  custodian of the child until a court of competent jurisdiction issues an order
  allocating parenting responsibilities for the child." Proposed R.C. 3109.0425 requires
  the best interest of the child to be "paramount" in such a case and forbids preference
  by financial status or gender. Proposed R.C. 3109.0426 carries forward the rape/sexual
  battery exclusion, with a "special circumstances" escape.
- **Enforcement.** Proposed R.C. 3109.0491 (motion alleging interference; five mandatory
  contents — award date, description, dates and amount of time lost, efforts made, relief
  requested; "the court shall hold an initial hearing not later than twenty-eight days
  after service unless for good cause shown"); 3109.0492 (interim protective orders);
  3109.0493 (on a finding of "unreasonable interference," the court **may** issue any of
  seven remedies (A)–(G), including a modified plan, compensatory parenting time,
  supervision, counseling, bond, costs and fees, and "any other remedy").
- **Relocation.** Proposed R.C. 3109.0470–3109.0479: 60-day advance notice (or within 10
  days of learning, if 60 is impossible), clerk service, safety-based withholding of the
  relocating party's address, objection motions, and a factor list aimed at "foster[ing] a
  continuing meaningful relationship between the child and nonrelocating party."
- **Records and access.** Proposed R.C. 3109.0516–3109.0529 recodify current
  R.C. 3109.051(H)–(J) and related sections and — importantly — "[r]emove[] distinctions
  between residential and nonresidential parents and expand[] access laws to all legal
  custodians."
- **Appeals.** Proposed R.C. 3109.0428: calendar priority and expeditious handling.
- **Transition — proposed R.C. 3109.0498.** Division (A) lets parties to a pre-effective
  date decree "file a motion … requesting the approval of a parenting plan." Division (B):
  a pre-effective-date decree "shall not be affected or invalidated by … the provisions of
  sections 3109.04 to 3109.0497" and "shall remain in full force and effect, subject to
  modification or termination pursuant to sections 3109.0418 to 3109.0420."
- **Child support.** New R.C. 3119.071 and 3119.072; definitional conforming changes;
  "[e]stablishes new provisions with regard to the effect of child custody on the
  calculation of child support after the bill's effective date."
- **Local rules.** Section 4 requests, on behalf of the General Assembly, that each court
  with domestic relations jurisdiction "review and update the court's local rules
  regarding parenting time to comply with the bill's provisions."

### C.4 The "special weight" defect

National Parents Organization's June 3, 2026 testimony identifies a real problem: the
House substitute imports *Troxel*'s "special weight" standard without defining it. The
relevant Ohio authority is **Harrold v. Collier**, 107 Ohio St.3d 44, 2005-Ohio-5334,
which noted that *Troxel* faulted the Washington court because "the trial court
disregarded the traditional presumption that a fit parent acts in the best interest of his
or her child," and **Oliver v. Feldner**, 149 Ohio App.3d 114, 2002-Ohio-3209 (7th Dist.),
¶ 23: "The 'special weight' requirement, as illuminated by these prior Supreme Court
cases, means that the deference provided to the parent's wishes will be overcome only by
some compelling governmental interest and overwhelmingly clear circumstances supporting
that governmental interest."

Two observations. First, if "special weight" carries its *Oliver v. Feldner* content, the
House substitute has arguably created something *stronger* than a preponderance
presumption for jointly agreed plans, without saying so — an invitation to appellate
litigation. Second, codifying the fit-parent presumption expressly (as NPO urges) is a
low-cost, high-value amendment: it changes no outcome the Constitution does not already
require, and it forecloses error. This proposal codifies it.

> (A) There is a rebuttable presumption that equal decision-making rights and
> responsibilities between the parents, with both parents remaining legal custodians and
> residential parents, is in the best interest of the children. This presumption is
> rebutted only if the court finds by **clear and convincing evidence**, based on the
> factors listed in section 3109.0421 of the Revised Code, that such an arrangement would
> be **detrimental** to the children. If a parent objects … that parent bears the burden of
> proof … If the court finds the presumption is rebutted, it shall issue findings of fact
> and conclusions of law supporting the determination.
>
> (B) There is a rebuttable presumption that equal parenting time is in the best interest
> of the children. This presumption is rebutted only if the court finds by **clear and
> convincing evidence**, based on the factors listed in section 3109.0422 of the Revised
> Code, that an equal parenting time arrangement would be **detrimental** to the minor
> children. If a parent objects to equal parenting time, that parent bears the burden of
> proof that such an arrangement would be detrimental to the minor children. If the court
> finds the presumption is rebutted, it shall issue findings of fact and conclusions of law
> supporting the determination."

Rebuttal factors: proposed R.C. 3109.0421 (decision-making: five factors, (A) demonstrated
ability to cooperate through (E) "[w]hether a parent is totally incapable of supporting or
caring for the child"); proposed R.C. 3109.0422 (parenting time: ten factors, including
"(B) The geographic proximity of the parents to each other at the time of initial filing,
as the proximity relates to the practical considerations of parenting time **and whether a
parent has relocated to impede equal parenting time**"; "(G) Whether a parent has
continuously and willfully interfered with or denied the other parent's right to parenting
time in accordance with an order of the court"; and "(J) Whether a parent is totally
incapable of supporting or caring for the child").

**Proposed R.C. 3109.0423** then required the court, *after* finding a presumption
rebutted, to determine whether a parent had intentionally "(A) Misled the court to cause
an unnecessary delay, increase the cost of litigation, or persuade the court to give that
parent a preference…; (B) Made false allegations against the other parent of harm to the
child; (C) Communicated false information to law enforcement, a public children services
agency, or the court in order to gain a tactical advantage." Proposed R.C. 3109.0424 and
3109.0425 then channeled the majority of time or decision-making to the parent who was
"more likely to honor and facilitate parenting time" and who had not done any of the
3109.0423 acts, and required the court to "[c]onstruct a parenting time schedule with the
child that is consistent with ensuring the child's welfare."

**Temporary orders — proposed R.C. 3109.0413(B)(2)(a)**, verbatim: "If requested by a
parent when the parents do not agree on the terms of a temporary allocation of parental
rights and responsibilities, the court shall provide in the temporary order equal
parenting time with the child, unless the court finds by clear and convincing evidence
that it would be detrimental to the child. If either parent objects to equal parenting
time, that parent bears the burden of proof that equal parenting time would be detrimental
to the child. If the court determines by clear and convincing evidence that equal parenting
time would be detrimental to the child, it shall issue findings of fact and conclusions of
law supporting the determination."

**Non-marital cases — proposed amendment to R.C. 3109.12(B)(1)**, verbatim: "With regard
to any order granting parenting time rights that is issued under this section, there is a
presumption that equal parenting time is in the best interest of the child, subject to the
factors set forth in section 3109.0453 and sections 3109.0454, 3109.0455, 3109.0456, and
3109.0457 of the Revised Code."

**What H.B. 14 did not do, and this is important:** it **left R.C. 3109.042(A) intact.**
Its amendment to that section changed only the cross-references and the verb ("designating
another person as the residential parent and legal custodian" → "allocating parental rights
and responsibilities … in accordance with sections 3109.04 to 3109.0445 …"). The unmarried
mother remained "the sole residential parent and legal custodian of the child until a court
of competent jurisdiction issues an order." So an unmarried father under H.B. 14 would have
had a presumption of equal parenting time — but only once he had filed a case and reached
an order. The legal-status gap was untouched.

### C.6 Why H.B. 14 failed, in the opponents' own words

**Ohio State Bar Association**, statement in opposition, Nicole Rutter-Hirth, House
Families and Aging Committee (Rep. Jean Schmidt, Chair), March 21, 2023. Her objections,
in her order, with her language:

1. **One size does not fit all.** "this bill requires the courts to treat cases in a
   one-size-fits-all approach. … Worse than that, it overshadows the best interest of the
   child standard used by courts for decades." Illustrations: equal care by "a parent with
   a drug or alcohol addiction or mental health issues"; forcing "a victim of domestic
   violence to co-parent with their abuser"; parents "dozens or hundreds of miles apart";
   third-shift workers versus stay-at-home parents.
2. **It undermines the judiciary.** "Mandating judicial action removes discretion from the
   elected officials. It tells voters that judges cannot be trusted…"
3. **It will *increase* litigation and cost — and the mechanism is the burden of proof.**
   "It raises the legal standard. The current standard of proof is a preponderance of the
   evidence… Clear and convincing evidence is elevated…" With specifics: psychological
   evaluations "6-12 months and cost upwards of $5-10K"; drug tests "$200-800"; continuous
   alcohol monitoring "a few hundred to a few thousand dollars" plus monthly fees; GALs
   "$1,500-2,000" and all but mandatory, in a market where "there are only three GALs on
   the court's appointment list."
4. **It disadvantages the vulnerable.** "Lower income families cannot afford to meet the
   burden of proof they're forced to meet. … This new standard will force victims of
   domestic violence to prove they were abused by clear and convincing evidence."
5. **The floodgate.** "setting the standard that all cases are appropriate for shared
   custody and equal time will certainly open a floodgate for all previously litigated
   custody cases. … Let's not forget that the current child support system reduces support
   based on the number of overnights the obligor has with the children. **There will be a
   financial incentive to litigate.**"
6. **Framing.** "The Equal Parenting bills shifts the focus. It requires proof that shared
   parenting is detrimental to the child instead of proof that the arrangement is in the
   best interest of the child."

Note what she concedes, twice and unprompted: "I am not opposed to equal rights when
appropriate. I have filed more shared parenting plans in the last three years than I did in
my first 14 years of practice. To be clear, I am not opposed to equal parenting schedules.
I'm proud to practice in several counties that offer week on/off parenting time as one of
their default schedules."

**Ohio Domestic Violence Network**, opponent testimony, same hearing (Bridget Mahoney and
Maria York; ODVN executive director Mary O'Doherty on the 2024 substitute). Reported
positions: the bill shifts Ohio "from a child-centric model to a parent-entitlement
model"; "The bill starts all families at the finish line"; and "The bill will create a
chilling effect on victims, who will be afraid to present evidence that the other parent is
unsafe." Other opponents included the Legal Aid Society of Cleveland and YWCA of Dayton.

**The November 2023 amendment and what it proves.** The House Families and Aging Committee
amended H.B. 14 to change the standard from clear and convincing to **preponderance** and
to change "equal" to "**substantially** equal," and added court inquiries and factors.
Resistance continued at the May 15, 2024 hearing. Proponents at that hearing included Rep.
Creech ("Now that I have my children 50% of the time, we don't fight over time any more"),
National Parents Organization, and individual parents.

**The five drafting conclusions this history compels, each adopted below:**

1. **Use preponderance.** Objection 3 and 4 — the most concrete, most quantified, and most
   sympathetic objections in the entire record — evaporate. And every enacted model except
   Arkansas already does it.
2. **Do not use "detrimental."** H.B. 14 asked the objecting parent to prove *detriment*, a
   higher and unfamiliar bar than "not in the best interest." Use "not in the best
   interest," which is the Florida and Kentucky formulation and preserves the best-interest
   frame Objection 1 and 6 defend.
3. **Do not reopen existing orders.** Objection 5 is a legitimate institutional concern and
   costs nothing to concede.
4. **Address geography and impracticability expressly.** Objection 1's most persuasive
   examples are distance cases. A maximize-time fallback plus an explicit impracticability
   ground answers them on the face of the statute.
5. **Keep judicial discretion where safety is at issue, and say so.** Objection 2 is
   really about tone. A bill that channels routine cases and expands the safety toolkit
   (rather than trimming it) is defensible to judges. The Ohio Judicial Conference, the
   Ohio Association of Domestic Relations Judges and the Ohio Association of Juvenile Court
   Judges all support S.B. 174; building on their bill rather than replacing it is the only
   viable posture.

### C.7 H.B. 508, 134th G.A. **[DID NOT PASS]**

Primary sponsors Reps. West and Creech; referred to House Civil Justice; did not pass. It
was the direct predecessor of H.B. 14: state policy of equal sharing; rebuttable
presumptions of equal decision-making and equal parenting time, rebuttable by clear and
convincing evidence; mandatory shared parenting order if the presumptions were not
rebutted; a factor list for rebuttal.

Two features of H.B. 508 are worth reviving and were dropped from H.B. 14:

1. **It required each court of common pleas to adopt standard parenting time guidelines
   "as established in the bill"** — i.e., a statewide minimum schedule, imposed through the
   existing R.C. 3109.051(F)(2) local-rule mechanism. That is precisely the Part IX
   architecture, and it is Ohio's own idea, not an import.
2. **It required an annual report of data on how parenting rights and responsibilities were
   actually divided.** This is the same "performance measurement" that National Parents
   Organization urged on the House Judiciary Committee in June 2026 and that S.B. 174 lacks.
   It is cheap, it is the only way anyone will ever know whether the reform worked, and it
   is politically almost unopposable.

### C.8 The political arithmetic, stated plainly

**Supporting S.B. 174** (from the Ohio State Bar Association's own December 16, 2025
one-pager, "more than 90 organizations"): Ohio Domestic Violence Network, Ohio State Bar
Association, Ohio Judicial Conference, Ohio Association of Domestic Relations Judges, Ohio
Association of Juvenile Court Judges, Ohio Association of Magistrates, American Academy of
Matrimonial Lawyers–Ohio Chapter, Ohio Alliance to End Sexual Violence, Ohio Poverty Law
Center, Ohio NOW, Groundwork Ohio, Ohio Crime Victim Justice Center, and roughly eighty
local domestic-violence and victim-services organizations.

**Opposing S.B. 174:** National Parents Organization; individual parents (Creed, Ayers,
Haas, Slosser, McNeese and others before Senate Judiciary on October 8 and November 5,
2025); and at least one domestic-violence survivor who asked the committee either to reject
the bill or to amend it to add "explicit accountability, stronger domestic violence
protections, and language that prevents any presumption of shared parenting in abuse
cases."

**The inversion is the central strategic fact of this project.** The coalition that
defeated H.B. 14 is the coalition that wrote and now defends S.B. 174. A free-standing
equal-parenting bill introduced in the 136th or 137th General Assembly would be opposed by
every organization on the supporting list and would fail exactly as H.B. 14 and H.B. 508
failed. An amendment to S.B. 174 — short, drafted in S.B. 174's own vocabulary, adopting
S.B. 174's own preponderance standard from proposed R.C. 3109.0455, using S.B. 174's own
28-day hearing clock, and importing the Kentucky domestic-violence architecture that the
domestic-violence organizations themselves would recognise — is a different proposition. It
asks the supporting coalition to accept one addition to a bill they already own, not to
surrender.

No recommendation is made here about individual legislators.

## PART D — INTERNATIONAL AND CONSTITUTIONAL RESEARCH ON "PARENTAL ALIENATION"

### D.1 Brazil — Law No. 12.318/2010, and why it is a cautionary tale

Brazil is the first and, on available research, the only country to define and sanction
"parental alienation" (*alienação parental*) as such by statute. **Article 2** defines it
as interference in the psychological formation of the child or adolescent, promoted or
induced by a parent, grandparent, or a person having the child under their authority,
custody or surveillance, so that a parent is repudiated by the child or the child's
relationship and contact with that parent is damaged. **Article 6** provides sanctions
applicable cumulatively or separately according to severity: warning, expanded visitation
for the alienated parent, fines, psychological counselling, change of custody, and
suspension of parental authority.

**What happened next is the point.** On 30 September 2025, the UN Special Rapporteur on
violence against women and girls publicly called on the Brazilian Parliament to repeal Law
12.318/2010, describing it as harmful. The stated basis: the law has been used against
mothers who came forward with credible allegations of domestic abuse, and international and
regional human rights mechanisms had repeatedly recommended repeal. Academic criticism runs
the same way — see *Fourteen years of the 'parental alienation Act' in Brazil: negative
impacts on the child custody decision-making process, children's best interests, and
women's rights*, Journal of Social Welfare and Family Law (2026). The core critique is that
the statute rests on a construct lacking scientific validity and operates in practice to
silence abuse allegations.

**The drafting lesson is exact and should be stated in sponsor testimony before anyone else
states it:** a statute that makes liability turn on a psychological construct —
"alienation," "alienating behaviours," "interference in psychological formation" — hands the
accused parent's advocate a mechanism for recharacterising a protective parent's safety
concerns as pathology. The remedy is not to abandon the policy. It is to make every element
of liability an **observable act**: a missed exchange, a concealed location, a specific false
statement made for a specific purpose. An act either happened or it did not, and the parent
who says it did not can prove it. A syndrome cannot be disproved.

This proposal therefore:

- never uses "parental alienation" or any cognate in operative text;
- names the conduct "**intentional interference with a parent-child relationship**";
- defines every qualifying act by reference to conduct and a stated purpose;
- requires a court order to exist before most of the acts can be committed; and
- protects good-faith abuse reporting expressly and unconditionally.

The popular term may of course be used descriptively in a preamble, a press release, or
testimony. It has no business in an element.

### D.2 Canada

The **Divorce Act**, R.S.C. 1985, c. 3 (2nd Supp.), as amended by S.C. 2019, c. 16
(in force March 1, 2021), took the opposite approach to Brazil: it addresses the same
problem entirely through best-interest factors and a maximum-time principle, with no
alienation concept and no offence.

- **Section 16(6):** "In allocating parenting time, the court shall give effect to the
  principle that a child should have as much time with each spouse as is consistent with
  the best interests of the child."
- **Section 16(3)(c):** among the best-interest factors, "each spouse's willingness to
  support the development and maintenance of the child's relationship with the other
  spouse."

Section 16(6) is the cleanest statement of the maximize-time principle in any
English-language statute and is functionally identical to W.Va. Code § 48-9-102a's second
sentence. It confirms that the maximize-time fallback is mainstream drafting, not an
advocacy artefact.

### D.3 Mexico and other jurisdictions

Several Mexican states have addressed *alienación parental* in their civil codes and, in
some instances, criminally. Research for this memorandum did not verify current Mexican
state texts against official sources, and **no Mexican provision is relied on or cited in
the draft.** That is a deliberate limit: the project brief forbids fabrication, and an
unverified foreign citation in sponsor testimony is a liability with no compensating
benefit. If a Mexican comparison is wanted, it should be sourced from the relevant state's
official *Periódico Oficial* before use.

### D.4 United States constitutional constraints on this kind of statute

The research relevant to Document 6, collected here:

- **Parental rights.** *Troxel v. Granville*, 530 U.S. 57 (2000) — the interest of parents
  in the care, custody and control of their children is "perhaps the oldest of the
  fundamental liberty interests"; there is a presumption that fit parents act in their
  children's best interests; a court must give "special weight" to a fit parent's
  determination. In Ohio: **Harrold v. Collier**, 107 Ohio St.3d 44, 2005-Ohio-5334
  (*Troxel* presumption is rebuttable; R.C. 3109.11 and 3109.12 upheld);
  **Oliver v. Feldner**, 149 Ohio App.3d 114, 2002-Ohio-3209 (7th Dist.) (content of
  "special weight").
- **Equal protection.** A rule that assigns sole legal custody to one parent by operation
  of law based on the parents' marital status — R.C. 3109.042(A), and proposed
  R.C. 3109.0424 — is the provision in this field most exposed to challenge, and it is
  exposed on two independent grounds: it classifies by marital status and, because it
  operates only on "an unmarried female who gives birth," it operates as a sex-based
  classification in effect. The safest course is not to defend it but to replace it with a
  parentage-based rule, which is both better policy and constitutionally inert.
- **First Amendment.** This is the live risk in any alienation provision, because several
  candidate acts are speech. **Shak v. Shak**, 484 Mass. 658 (2020), held a
  non-disparagement order in a custody case an unconstitutional prior restraint. The
  general rule: prior restraints are permissible "only where the harm expected from the
  unrestrained speech is grave, the likelihood of harm occurring without the prior
  restraint in place is all but certain, and there are no alternative, less restrictive
  means to mitigate the harm." Academic treatment: Eugene Volokh, *Parent-Child Speech and
  Child Custody Speech Restrictions*, 81 N.Y.U. L. Rev. 631 (2006).
  **Implication for drafting:** do not prohibit disparagement, criticism, or negative
  statements about the other parent. Reach speech only where it is (a) a knowingly false
  statement of *fact*, (b) material, (c) made for the purpose of defeating a court order,
  or (d) a direction to a child to violate a court order. Knowingly false factual
  statements and speech integral to a course of unlawful conduct are the two categories
  that carry the least First Amendment protection.
- **Vagueness.** Criminal statutes must give ordinary people fair notice and must not
  invite arbitrary enforcement, and the doctrine applies with special force where First
  Amendment interests are at stake because of the chilling effect. "Course of conduct,"
  "substantially interferes," and "without lawful justification" are all terms that have
  been sustained in other contexts but each needs an anchor. The anchors used here are: a
  numerical trigger (three denials in 90 days), an existing court order as the reference
  point, and a closed list of safe harbours.
- **Mandatory presumptions in criminal cases.** Note the distinction: the equal-parenting
  presumption is a civil burden-allocation device and raises no *Sandstrom/Francis* problem.
  Nothing in the criminal section may presume any element from proof of another; the
  three-denial trigger must be drafted as an element the State proves, not as a presumption
  of intent.

## PART E — CONSOLIDATED DRAFTING RECOMMENDATIONS

1. **Vehicle:** an amendment to S.B. 174 in House Judiciary, with a free-standing companion
   bill drafted against current law as the fallback if S.B. 174 dies. Draft once, in
   S.B. 174's vocabulary, with a contingency section.
2. **Standard:** preponderance of the evidence; "not in the best interest of the child";
   never "detrimental."
3. **Presumption:** one sentence, on the West Virginia model, covering parenting time and
   major decision-making separately (per A.R.S. § 25-403.02, they are not the same thing).
4. **Scope:** initial permanent orders and temporary orders; divorce, dissolution, legal
   separation, annulment, and non-marital parentage cases alike. Not modifications of
   pre-existing orders.
5. **Fallback:** mandatory maximize-time schedule (W.Va. § 48-9-102a; KRS 403.270(2);
   Divorce Act (Canada) s. 16(6)).
6. **Floor:** a statewide statutory minimum on the Utah § 30-3-35 model, set at the level of
   the least generous existing Franklin County option, enforced through the existing
   R.C. 3109.051(F)(2) local-rule mandate (the H.B. 508 device). Departure below the floor
   only on the S.B. 174 proposed R.C. 3109.0455 preponderance standard with least
   restrictive means.
7. **Unmarried parents:** replace the sole-custodian rule with equal parental status on
   final establishment of parentage; the statutory floor applies as the interim schedule
   until an order issues; exceptions for a protection order, a rape or sexual battery
   conviction, and immediate substantial risk.
8. **Safety:** import the Kentucky 2026 architecture — front-loaded consideration of abuse
   and domestic-violence allegations with written findings before other factors; no
   presumption against a party protected by a domestic violence order; reverse presumption
   on two or more found acts of domestic violence; and the anti-weaponization clause of
   KRS 403.270(4)(l).
9. **Enforcement:** S.B. 174's 28-day clock; mandatory make-up time (House substitute;
   Tex. Fam. Code § 157.168(a)); escalation on the Minnesota subd. 6 ladder; three contempt
   findings → material change (Tex. Fam. Code § 156.107), doubled make-up time
   (§ 157.168(d)), and no fee waiver (§ 157.167(e)).
10. **Criminal:** a new narrow offence in Chapter 2919 built on the R.C. 2919.231 template
    and the Illinois 720 ILCS 5/10-5.5 conduct formulation; three denials in 90 days;
    misdemeanor of the first degree, felony of the fifth degree on the R.C. 2919.23(D)(2)
    aggravators; Minnesota § 609.26 safe harbours; express non-exclusivity clause codifying
    *Toledo v. Parra* ¶¶ 17–18. **Number it R.C. 2919.232 — R.C. 2919.231 is occupied.**
11. **False allegations:** the KRS 403.270(4)(h) triple guard; affirmative protection for
    good-faith reports; express statement that an unsubstantiated, unprosecuted, unproven,
    or mistaken report is not a violation; preserve R.C. 2151.421 reporting immunity.
12. **Transition:** prospective only; enactment is not a change in circumstances; no new
    modification pathway beyond what S.B. 174 proposed R.C. 3109.0498(A) already creates.
13. **Child support:** technical only. State that equal parenting time does not itself
    eliminate a support obligation, and conform to R.C. 3119.051 and 3119.231. Do not touch
    the schedule or the worksheet.
14. **Add the H.B. 508 data report.** It costs nothing, it answers the "untested mechanism"
    objection from both directions, and it is the only provision in this bill that both
    National Parents Organization and the Ohio Judicial Conference have separately asked for.
