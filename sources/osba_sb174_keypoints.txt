                              SB 174 (Gavarone, Hicks-Hudson)
                              An Overdue Update That Puts Children and Parents First

Children thrive when both parents are supported and play an active role in their lives.
SB 174 ensures that courts have clear, updated standards—reinforced with transparency
and accountability—to help advance these principles.


                                  An Overdue Update That Has Been 25 Years
                                  in the Making
                            • Ohio’s parenting laws have not undergone a meaningful update in
                              generations; SB 174 finally brings them in line with today’s family realities
                              and child-development research.
                            • It is the product of decades of bipartisan work, beginning with the 1999 Ohio Task
                              Force on Family Law and Children and continuing through years of refinement by
                              judges, practitioners, children’s advocates and statewide stakeholders.

                                  Legislation That Strengthens, Not Weakens,
                                  the Role of Parents
                            • SB 174 explicitly reinforces the longstanding principle that children benefit from
                              meaningful relationships with both parents, with a priority on children’s safety and
                              best interests. Courts cannot give preference to a parent because of their financial
                              status or gender.
                            • Parental authority is preserved. Parents can submit their own agreed parenting
                              plan, separate plans for judicial comparison or rely on the judge if they cannot
                              agree. Judges do not displace parents—they step in only when parents are in
                              conflict or when a child’s safety or best interest demands it.
                            • Under SB 174, parents have a new pathway to increase their parenting time.
                              Current law makes it hard for parents whose children do not live with to revise
                              parenting plans and increase their parenting time. This bill improves that by
                              creating a new, easier process for revising parenting plans so children can more
                              easily benefit from having more time with both parents.

                                  The Best Interests of Children Remain
                                  at the Center of Decisions
                            • SB 174 clarifies and expands the “best interest” factors so that courts evaluate
                              cases based on real-world needs: a child’s safety, stability, developmental
                              readiness, parents’ cooperation and each parent’s ability to meet daily needs. It
                              also restricts from considering one “best interest” factor to the exclusion of others.
                            • This is not a shift toward government control—it is a clarification of the child-
                              focused standards courts must already follow.

                                  The Role of Judges Is Defined When
                                  Parents Cannot Agree
                            • Judges are not given new arbitrary authority. Instead, their role is defined, with
                              increased transparency, guardrails and accountability for those instances when
                              parents cannot agree.
                            • Equal parenting time is preferred, and if a judge denies a request, they must now
                              explain why—in writing—based on safety, best interest or other good cause. This
                              reduces and limits judicial discretion, not expands it.
                            • Contrary to criticisms, SB 174 does not impose a single, state-designed
                              parenting plan. Parents remain fully empowered to craft individualized plans—
                              courts intervene only to protect children’s best interests or when parents cannot
                              reach agreement.
