Chairperson Thomas, Vice Chair Swearingen, Ranking Member Synenberg and members
of the House Judiciary Committee, thank you for the opportunity to provide opponent
testimony on SB174.

My name is Don Hubin, I’m an Emeritus Professor of Philosophy at the Ohio State
University and the Founding Director Emeritus of the OSU Center for Ethics and Human
Values. I’m also the Chair of the National Board of National Parents Organization. NPO is
the nation’s largest and most effective shared parenting organization, with chapters in 36
states. We work to promote children’s best interest by strengthening the bonds between
children and their fit and loving parents when those parents live apart.

According to proponents of SB174, the bill seeks to enact into law the recommendations
of the 2001 Ohio Task Force on Family Law and Children and the 2005 recommendations
of the Supreme Court of Ohio’s Advisory Committee on Children, Families & the Courts, a
body to which I was invited to testify these many years ago. Proponents point to the
objective of reducing the adversarial nature of decisions about parental responsibilities
and encouraging parents to share in the responsibilities of raising their children and
courts to maximize the time the children have with each parent.

These are fine objectives; NPO fully endorses the goals of SB174. But we believe that the
methods SB174 seeks to employ to achieve these goals are inadequate and, in some cases,
counterproductive.

In previous testimony on the bill—testimony before the Senate Judiciary Committee—I
noted the positive elements of the bill and focused my comments on the serious problems
with the bill. Some of those problems have been addressed in the substitute bill now
before the Committee. However—and I want to emphasize this—despite the positive
Hubin Testimony in Opposition to SB 174 (6/3/2026)​                                      ​ p. 2


rhetoric in the bill and the lofty goals of the sponsors and proponents, this bill is still
seriously flawed because it maintains a system of total judicial subjectivity. By refusing to
establish a clear legal baseline, it guarantees that custody battles remain a high-stakes
gamble. In the absence of a presumption that both parents will remain fully and equally
engaged in their children’s lives, parents fear the worst and respond accordingly. The best
defense is often a good offense, so some parents, sometimes at the urging of their
attorneys, will lead with everything bad they can think of about the other parent. We
should not be surprised that parents fight when we’ve set up for them a Hunger Games
approach to post-separation parenting arrangements.

Proponents of this bill, apparently motivated in large part by the desire to forestall the
prospect of a true equal shared parenting bill, describe equal shared parenting
presumptions as creating “a rigid mandate” or “automatically awarding equal parenting
time.” This is ridiculous on its face, of course. Such presumptions are rebuttable and the
court retains discretion to issue orders to protect both children and parents from harm.
Sponsors of this bill testified that “50/50 is the starting point of this legislation.” But it’s
not. You establish a starting point by creating a rebuttable presumption. And SB174 does
not create a presumption that equal shared parenting is in the best interest of the child.
Instead, it denies that there is any starting point.

Proponents of this bill apparently believe that the positive language in the bill, together
with a requirement that courts must give their reasons for rejecting a request for equal
parenting time, will change the behavior of courts in making custody decisions. There are
reasons for great skepticism.

   ●​ Cultural Inertia: 25 years after this General Assembly replaced “visitation” with
      “parenting time,” 30 Ohio counties still use the outdated, offensive language that
      reflects a mindset that the child is raised by one parent and only visits the other.
   ●​ Existing Default Orders: 45 Ohio counties still rely on archaic “every other
      weekend” default schedules, treating one fit parent as a mere visitor.

Some of those 45 counties didn’t even say that these schedules apply “in the absence of
parental agreement” to a different schedule. They simply informed parents, many of
whom are not represented by counsel, that “parenting time [or often “visitation”] shall be
as follows …” Presumably, all of these 45 courts believe that the “every other weekend”
schedules are typically best for children or they wouldn’t still provide them as default
schedules. Why should anyone think that these courts will have a change of heart because
of the positive language in SB174? Why should anyone think that the mere requirement to
give a reason for overriding a request for equal shared parenting, even a joint request
Hubin Testimony in Opposition to SB 174 (6/3/2026)​                                         ​ p. 3


from both parents, is going to change these judges' minds about what custody orders to
impose on families.

Another reason for skepticism is this. The two reports that proponents of SB174 claim
motivated the bill were published in 2001 and 2005. Proponents would have us believe
that the Ohio Bar Association and the Ohio Association of Domestic Relations Judges
were assiduously studying these issues for a quarter of a century and, after all that hard
work, produced SB174. But it really looks more like these family law experts spent about
20 years ignoring the recommendations in these reports and the last five working to head
off a change in Ohio law to create a true equal shared parenting starting point.

 Opponents of the bill, including Representative Williams who initially opposed it,
questioned the constitutionality of several provisions of the bill. Changes were made to
address some of these concerns. But some remain. In order to try to bring the bill in line
with the U.S. Supreme Court’s decision in Troxel v. Granville (530 U.S. 57 (2000)), the bill has
been amended to require courts to give “special weight to the joint parenting plan filed by
the parents or legal custodians” (§3109.046(A)). Unfortunately, the bill fails to define
“special weight”.

There is, though, case law on this matter. In Harrold v. Collier (107 Ohio St.3d 44,
2005-Ohio-5334), the Ohio Supreme Court noted that the U.S. The Supreme Court
overruled the State of Washington’s decision in the Troxel case because “the trial court
disregarded the traditional presumption that a fit parent acts in the best interest of his or her
child” (emphasis added). Prior to that decision, the Seventh District Court of Appeals of
Ohio ruled:

       The “special weight” requirement, as illuminated by these prior Supreme
       Court cases, means that the deference provided to the parent’s wishes will
       be overcome only by some compelling governmental interest and
       overwhelmingly clear circumstances supporting that governmental
       interest. (Oliver v. Feldner, 149 Ohio App.3d 114 2002-Ohio-3209, at p. 23)

 Sponsors of SB174 have nevertheless resisted adding to the bill an explicit presumption
that fit parents act in the best interest of their children. One might think that this omission
doesn’t matter because the U.S. Supreme Court has the final word on what is consistent
with the constitution and, so, the presumption that fit parents act in the best interest of
their children is still the law of the land. But that would be naive. It does matter. Leaving
'special weight' undefined in the statute is a recipe for endless appellate litigation.
Explicitly codifying the presumption that fit parents act in their children's best interests
doesn't create new law—it prevents Ohio judges from committing constitutional errors
that will ultimately be overturned on appeal.

A Model for the Country?

In sponsor testimony, Representative Tims asked Senator Gavarone if there were
examples of laws like this in other states. Senator Gavarone said she knew of none. Nor do
we.

Meanwhile, we have hard, multi-year data from states that enacted a true, rebuttable
presumption of equal shared parenting. The results are not minor; they are staggering.
The states for which we have multiple years of data have experienced drops—sometimes
dramatic drops—in child maltreatment.

States that adopted presumptions of equal shared parenting have also seen drops in the
rates of intimate partner violence. The Supreme Court of Appeals of West Virginia Annual
Statistics Report on Circuit, Family, and Magistrate Court Judges, reports that between 2021
(the year before the law took effect) and 2024, the number of cases of domestic violence
filed in family courts dropped by more than 4% from 8,979 to 8,606. In Arkansas, those
counties that reported domestic violence cases saw a drop of more than 11% between the
year before enactment and 2024. These are statistics about overall domestic violence
cases—many of which are unrelated to divorce. In Kentucky, we have compelling evidence
from the Kentucky Administrative Office of the Courts. It crosslisted Domestic & Family
cases with Domestic Violence cases. The data are dramatic. There were 979 such cases in
2017. Since the enactment of Kentucky’s equal shared parenting law, that has trended
downward and now stands at 290. This is a drop of more than 70%!

Ohio could learn from these states’ experiences. However, proponents of SB174 ask you
to take a blind bet on an untested, homegrown mechanism. Senator Gavarone noted that,
while there are no similar laws in existence, the bill is the “culmination of 20 years of work
and more than a hundred people … and this legislation should be the model if you look at
what this does“ (emphasis added). We agree that if this bill is enacted into law, we should
look at what it does. That is different, though, from looking at what proponents hope or
anticipate it will do.

While we think it wiser to follow tested approaches to revising family law to promote
children’s best interest by keeping both parents fully engaged in raising them, it appears,
that the Ohio legislature is on a course to take this bet on the untested approach. And if
that is correct, we have a final recommendation we urge the House to take very seriously.

   ●​ Performance Measurement: Include in the legislation a requirement to generate and
      evaluate metrics for success.

What are the positive outcomes proponents tout that we should measure?

   ●​ Reduced Parental Conflict: We should measure the rate of cases that are settled
      without litigation and the time and cost of litigation. If SB174 is successful in
      achieving its goals, these should go down.
   ●​ Increased Shared Physical Custody: We should measure the frequency with which
      parents are operating under a substantially equal parenting time schedule. We
Hubin Testimony in Opposition to SB 174 (6/3/2026)​                                    ​ p. 6


      know that children typically do best under this arrangement and, if SB174 is
      successful, we should expect the frequency of such arrangements to increase.
   ●​ Reduced Family Violence: If SB174 truly reduces parental conflict and focuses all
      parties on the best interest of the child, we should expect it to help reduce the
      family violence that sometimes arises for the first time during parental separation
      and custody battles. We should track this and see how Ohio compares with
      favorable results of those states that have adopted presumptions of equal shared
      parenting.

The most important reason for tracking these metrics is that they bear directly on the
well-being of children and their parents. There is also, though, a fiscal reason to track this.
According to the bill’s Fiscal Note & Local Impact Statement, the bill will impose costs on
courts of common pleas and possibly on child support enforcement agencies as well as
decreasing fee revenue. Responsible management of taxpayer dollars mandates that
there be metrics in place to determine whether this legislation, which is untried anywhere
in the country, is successful in achieving its declared ends. It is only after we can see, and
verify what this legislation actually does that it can serve as a model for the nation.

Chairperson Thomas and members of the committee, thank you again for allowing me to
provide testimony in opposition to SB174.
