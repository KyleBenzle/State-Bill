## 1. Restoring equal status under an existing order

**Attack (retroactivity).** "Article II, § 28 forbids retroactive laws. Mother has held sole custody
under a final decree for eight years. Letting father convert it to shared parenting without showing
any change in circumstances strips a vested right adjudicated in her favor."

**Answer.**
- **Ohio's test.** A statute is unconstitutionally retroactive only if the General Assembly made it
  retroactive *and* it is substantive, meaning it impairs vested rights, creates new obligations, or
  attaches new disabilities to past transactions (*Van Fossen v. Babcock & Wilcox Co.*, 36 Ohio
  St.3d 100 (1988); *Bielat v. Bielat*, 87 Ohio St.3d 350 (2000)).
- **No vested right.** A custody decree is not a vested right. It is, by statute, always subject to
  modification on the child's best interest. The court keeps continuing jurisdiction (R.C. 3109.04(E);
  3109.051(D) as read in *Braatz v. Braatz*, 85 Ohio St.3d 40 (1999)).
- **The rule operates prospectively.** It changes nothing until a motion is filed and granted, and
  then only from that date. It applies a new modification standard to future proceedings, which the
  General Assembly does whenever it amends 3109.04(E).
- **Precedent for this kind of statute.** The change-in-circumstances requirement is itself
  statutory. What the legislature created, it may relax for a defined class of motions.

**Attack (procedural due process).** "Mother's fundamental parental interest is reduced (she loses
sole decision-making) without an individualized best-interest determination."

**Answer.**
- She gets notice and an opportunity for a hearing.
- The court decides the three statutory grounds on evidence.
- The change she suffers is to **share** decision-making with another fit legal parent who holds the
  same fundamental interest. *Troxel* protects a parent against the state and third parties. It
  supplies no rule that one fit parent's interest outranks the other's.
- Where the two fit parents conflict on a major decision, § 3109.044(G) keeps the existing
  arrangement until the court decides. Her practical position is therefore protected by default.

**Attack (best interest).** "The statute commands 'shall grant' without any best-interest finding."

**Answer and fix.** This is the strongest point.
- It is answered by the narrowness of what is granted: **status only**, with schedule, support, and
  school frozen.
- The best-interest question is preserved where it bites, in every later schedule or decision
  dispute.
- **Optional hardening:** add a fourth ground to (E)(1)(c): "(iv) the court finds that shared
  decision-making would endanger the child." It is not added by default. A broad best-interest
  escape hatch would let courts deny status on the same grounds they deny it today. If added, it
  must be tied to danger, not preference.

**Residual risk: low-moderate.**

## 2. Restoring status vs. changing the schedule

**Attack.** "The father hears 'equal custodian' and shows up on a Saturday he has no time. Police
are confused. Exchanges become flashpoints."

**Answer.** The text answers it three times:
- 3109.042(B) (status is not a schedule);
- 3109.04(E)(1)(c) ("does not entitle either parent to have the child at any time not provided for
  in the existing schedule");
- 3109.045(A) (the order stays in effect as written).

The new decree **contains** the old schedule as its terms. That is the document an officer will be
shown. Self-help remains what it is today: contempt and, if knowing and repeated, 2919.23(E).

**Residual risk: low.** The only real risk is public misunderstanding. The plain-English summary
leads with "Equal status is not a schedule."

## 3. Unmarried parents before any order

**Attack (domestic-violence advocates).** "Making an unmarried father a legal custodian at the
moment paternity is acknowledged, before any court has looked at him, lets him take an infant from
the mother and refuse to return it. Today she is protected by 3109.042(A)."

**Answer.**
- That is exactly the legal position of every married mother before divorce. Ohio has never treated
  that as a constitutional or safety emergency, because remedies exist:
  - ex parte temporary orders on affidavit (R.C. 3109.043; Civ.R. 75(N));
  - protection orders (R.C. 3113.31, ex parte the same day);
  - emergency custody (R.C. 2151.31).
- Treating unmarried parents like married parents is the principle. The current rule's marital
  classification, applied by sex ("an unmarried female who gives birth"), is the bill's
  equal-protection *reduction*, not its exposure. Revision 1, Document 6 § 2 made this argument and
  it stands.

**Fix to consider (sponsor choice).** A no-removal rule pending a first order: "Until a court issues
an order, neither parent shall remove the child from the other parent's care without consent,
except to protect the child from imminent harm." Two observations:
- It would answer the grab-and-go objection directly.
- It would also be a new rule that married parents do not have. If added, add it in 3109.03, for
  all parents, to keep the equality principle intact.

**Reliance and transition.** Existing orders are not altered (3109.042(C)). The status change
applies automatically only where no custody or parenting-time order exists.

**Residual risk: low-moderate.** The risk is political, not legal.

## 4. The presumption and the floor

**Attack.** "A presumption of a particular schedule replaces individualized determination."

**Answer.**
- Five states have enacted the same device (KY 2018, AR 2021, WV 2022, FL 2023, MO 2023). Revision 1's
  research found no decision invalidating any of them.
- A presumption allocates the burden; it decides nothing.
- It is rebuttable on the lowest civil standard, using every existing best-interest factor.
- It does not apply on agreement, protection orders, or 3109.04(C) offenses.

**The floor.** 90 overnights, with four exceptions including endangerment, and least-restrictive
means. It is no more rigid than the existing statutory threshold in R.C. 3119.051 and is more
flexible than Utah's enacted hour-specific schedule.

**Residual risk: low.**

## 5. Only a court changes an order; GAL and parenting-coordinator authority

**Attack (separation of powers).** "GALs and parenting coordinators are governed by the Supreme
Court's Rules of Superintendence (Sup.R. 8.00–8.07 and 6.40–6.46, formerly 48 and 90). The General
Assembly cannot regulate court personnel."

**Answer.**
- **It works within the rules, not against them.** § 3109.045(B) and (C) say GALs and parenting
  coordinators act "as provided by law and the Rules of Superintendence." The Rules of
  Superintendence are administrative rules for court management. They are not Art. IV § 5(B) rules
  of practice and procedure, and they do not create substantive rights.
- **Who holds the authority is substantive.** Whether a non-judge can change a parent's court-ordered
  rights is a question of substantive law about parental rights. That is the General Assembly's to
  decide.
- **It codifies principles courts already state.** "The trial court may not delegate judicial
  authority" (*Weisgarber*). Due process requires independent review of coordinator decisions
  (*Gregory*). The statute reverses nothing a court has authorized (Document 1, § 4).
- **It reflects the Rules' own design.** Sup.R. 8.06(A)(3) says GAL reports are not
  "determinative," and Sup.R. 6.42(B) bars parenting coordination for "[s]ubstantive changes in
  parenting time." § 3109.045(B)–(C) restate those limits in statute.
- **Cross-examination is a due-process rule, not court administration.** *In re Hoffman* recognized
  it in permanent custody. Extending it to Chapter 3109 cases is substantive.

**Attack (child safety).** "A GAL who sees danger must be able to act now."

**Answer.** A GAL can do what any person can:
- move the court for an emergency or ex parte order;
- call law enforcement or children services, which can act under R.C. 2151.31.

§ 3109.045(E) preserves all of that. What the GAL cannot do is be the order.

**Attack (children services).** "Safety plans are how agencies keep children safe without court
involvement. Requiring consent or a court order for every plan that touches parenting time burdens
agencies."

**Answer.**
- Safety plans are, by design, voluntary agreements. A plan that a non-consenting parent is bound by
  is, in substance, an order without a judge. The provision requires only what the plan's voluntary
  character already implies.
- Where danger is real, Chapter 2151 provides emergency custody and ex parte orders.

**Residual risk: low.** Expect institutional opposition from agencies, not a constitutional
challenge.

## 6. The 36-month allegation limit

This is the most novel provision and the one most likely to be attacked by domestic-violence
organizations. It deserves the most care.

**Attack 1 (child safety).** "A real abuser benefits from court delay. After 36 months the
restriction simply ends and the child goes back to an unsafe parent because the court was slow."

**Answer.** The structure makes the outcome turn on whether the *court* decided, not on whether
the accuser proved:
- Either party may force a hearing within 90 days (§ 3109.046(C)).
- A court that has had a restriction in place for three years has had ample opportunity to decide
  the allegation, on the lowest civil standard. If it finds the allegation true, the restriction
  continues indefinitely (§ 3109.046(E)).
- The limit punishes only the *absence of any decision*.
- New conduct supports a new restriction.
- Protection orders and Chapter 2151 abuse cases, the primary safety tools, are excluded.
- § 3109.045(E) preserves emergency action at any time.

**Fix included.** The court may grant one continuance of up to 90 days on written findings.

**Fix included.** At 30 months the court must set the allegation for hearing on its own motion.
This converts the deadline from a cliff into a calendar entry.

**Attack 2 (due process for the restricted parent).** "Thirty-six months is far too long. A parent
can lose three years of a child's life on an unproven accusation."

**Answer.** Agreed. That is why the 90-day hearing on request is the operative mechanism, and 36
months is only the backstop. The restricted parent controls the timeline.

**Attack 3 (separation of powers).** "The legislature cannot dictate how fast courts decide cases."

**Answer.**
- The General Assembly routinely sets case-processing deadlines with consequences in this same
  field. R.C. 2151.35(B)(1) requires dismissal of an abuse complaint if disposition is not reached
  within 90 days plus one 45-day extension.
- § 3109.046 is milder: the consequence is that a restriction expires, not that the case is
  dismissed.
- Speedy-trial statutes (R.C. 2945.71) are the criminal analog and have long been upheld.

**Attack 4 (the definition of "resolved").** "An acquittal should count. And a 'substantiated'
agency finding should count."

**Answer.**
- An acquittal establishes only that guilt was not proven beyond a reasonable doubt, a standard
  higher than any civil court uses.
- A PCSA "substantiated" disposition can rest on "other forms of confirmation deemed valid by the
  PCSA" (Ohio Adm.Code 5180:2-1-01). It is an administrative label, not a hearing.
- Neither is a finding, after a hearing at which the restricted parent could be heard, on whether
  the allegation is true. Both remain admissible evidence in the hearing that does resolve the
  allegation.

**Residual risk: low-moderate.** Not because it is unconstitutional, but because it is new. The
30-month self-setting rule is what brings it down from moderate.

## 7. Knowingly false allegations

**Attack.** "Any sanction for 'false' allegations chills victims from reporting."

**Answer.**
- Liability requires all three of: **material falsity, actual knowledge, and purpose to interfere.**
- Unsubstantiated, unprosecuted, unproven, and mistaken reports are expressly not false.
- Good-faith reports may not be held against the reporter at all.
- R.C. 2151.421(H) reporting immunity is preserved.
- The consequences are restorative (make-up time, fees) and evidentiary (change in circumstances).
  They are not punitive.

**Optional hardening.** A clear-and-convincing standard (Design Memo, Option 2).

**Residual risk: low-moderate.**

## 8. Enforcement

- **Prima facie rule.** It shifts the burden of *producing evidence* of a lawful excuse, which is
  within the responding parent's knowledge, and expressly not the burden of persuasion. That is
  ordinary civil practice. Ohio civil contempt still requires clear and convincing proof (*Brown v.
  Executive 200, Inc.*, 64 Ohio St.2d 250 (1980)).
- **28-day hearing.** It mirrors Civ.R. 75(N)(2)'s existing 28-day temporary-order hearing. There is
  no conflict with a Civil Rule, so Art. IV § 5(B) supersession does not arise. If a court treated it
  as procedural, it would at worst be directory.
- **Mandatory make-up time.** It keeps a best-interest escape with written reasons.

**Residual risk: low.**

## 9. The criminal division, 2919.23(E)

**Vagueness.** The elements are:
- knowing conduct;
- a court order that allocates the time;
- a numerical threshold (3 in 90 days; 14 consecutive days; any after a contempt finding within 2
  years);
- the absence of an enumerated lawful excuse, which the state must disprove beyond a reasonable
  doubt.

A reasonable reading of an unclear order is a defense. This is more definite than the existing
"entice, take, keep, or harbor" that *Parra* had to construe.

**Mens rea.** "Knowingly" (R.C. 2901.22(B)), replacing (A)'s "knowing … or being reckless."

**Speech.** None. The offense reaches keeping a child, not statements.

**Narrowing *Parra*.** Channeling simple withholding into (E) removes single-incident liability.
This raises no constitutional issue, only a policy one (Design Memo, Option 3).

**Residual risk: low.** The Revision 1 version was low-moderate.

## 10. Prosecutor notice, 2919.23(G)

**Attack.** "The charging decision belongs to the executive. A statutory deadline pressures charging
and invades prosecutorial discretion."

**Answer.**
- *State ex rel. Master v. Cleveland*, 75 Ohio St.3d 23, 27 (1996): "the decision whether to
  prosecute is discretionary, and not generally subject to judicial review." *State ex rel. Rankin v.
  State*, 2025-Ohio-4483: "a prosecutor has no clear duty to prosecute."
- § 2919.23(G) is written around those holdings:
  - **It imposes a ministerial duty only:** file, or send a notice.
  - **No reasons are required**, so the substance of the decision is never exposed to review.
  - **The extension is self-executing** by the prosecutor's own notice. No court grants or denies it,
    so no judge supervises charging.
  - **A declination is expressly unreviewable and is no bar** to later prosecution.
- Prosecutors are statutory officers whose duties the General Assembly defines (R.C. Chapter 309),
  and it already imposes notice and confer duties on them (R.C. 2930.05(B)(2), 2930.06).
- **Enforcement of the duty.** If a prosecutor sends nothing, the remedy is mandamus to compel the
  *notice*. Mandamus lies for a clear ministerial duty. It does not lie to compel *prosecution*,
  which remains discretionary.
- **Practical cost.** One form letter.

**Residual risk: low.**

## 11. Protection of genuine abuse victims (whole bill)

The bill's safety architecture, gathered in one place:

1. The presumption does not apply to protection-order respondents or 3109.04(C) offenders.
2. A conviction is never required to protect a child (§ 3109.044(H)).
3. Courts may restrict on a preponderance (§ 3109.044(H)); the least restrictive means applies only
   below the floor.
4. Good-faith reports are protected absolutely; "unsubstantiated" never means "false."
5. A protective parent's good-faith action is not "failure to facilitate."
6. Lawful excuse covers good-faith protective action that is promptly reported, and compliance with
   protection orders.
7. Protection orders, Chapter 2151, R.C. 2151.31 emergency custody, and 2151.421 immunity are all
   preserved.
8. The criminal division requires the state to disprove every lawful excuse beyond a reasonable
   doubt.
9. No psychological syndrome or "alienation" concept appears anywhere. That is the Brazil lesson
   from Revision 1, Document 1 § D.1.

**The honest residual concerns:**
- the pre-order position of unmarried mothers (Part 3);
- the 36-month limit (Part 6), mitigated by the 30-month self-setting rule.

The first has a one-sentence fix available to the sponsor.

## 12. Single subject

The common subject is the allocation, protection, and enforcement of parental rights and parenting
time. One criminal division and one prosecutor-notice division serve that subject directly. Ohio
enforces Art. II § 15(D) only against manifestly gross and fraudulent violations.

**Residual risk: low.**
