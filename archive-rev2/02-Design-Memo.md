# DOCUMENT 2 — DESIGN MEMO (REVISION 2)

**What changed from Revision 1, and why.** 4 October 2026.

---

## Part 1. The one-paragraph version

Revision 1 was a 10,000-word overlay on S.B. 174. It enacted nineteen new sections, wrote a
second set of rebuttal grounds, an hour-by-hour minimum schedule, a civil "interference" tort,
a three-rung enforcement ladder, and a free-standing twelve-division crime.

Revision 2 is drafted against **current Ohio law** and runs about 4,600 words (Revision 1: about 10,100). It amends eight
existing sections and enacts three short new ones. Almost every mechanism it uses already exists
in the Revised Code:

- the equality sentence in R.C. 3109.03;
- the shared-parenting status rule in R.C. 3109.04(L)(6), under which each parent is a
  "residential parent and legal custodian … regardless of where the child is physically located";
- the existing motion to convert a sole-custody decree to shared parenting, R.C. 3109.04(E)(1)(b);
- the temporary-order statute, R.C. 3109.043, and Civil Rule 75(N)'s 28-day hearing;
- the parenting-time contempt action, R.C. 2705.031, and its graduated penalties, R.C. 2705.05;
- the existing make-up-time provision, R.C. 3109.051(K);
- and the interference-with-custody offense, R.C. 2919.23, as read in *Toledo v. Parra*.

What is genuinely new is small:

- equal status for unmarried parents;
- a 50/50 presumption;
- a no-change-in-circumstances route back to equal status;
- a rule that only a court changes a court order;
- a 36-month outside limit on allegation-based restrictions;
- a prosecutor-notice rule.

## Part 2. The test applied to every provision

Each provision had to answer four questions:

1. What specific problem does it solve?
2. Is a new statute actually needed?
3. Can existing Ohio machinery do it more simply?
4. Can a parent, officer, prosecutor, lawyer, or judge understand it without reading five other
   sections?

Applying the test removed roughly two-thirds of Revision 1. Document 8 lists every cut.

## Part 3. Why the bill is now drafted against current law, not S.B. 174

**Revision 1's reasoning:** S.B. 174's supporting coalition (the Ohio State Bar Association, the
judges' associations, and the Ohio Domestic Violence Network) is the coalition that killed
H.B. 14. So the earlier draft rode inside their bill.

**Why that is reversed:**

1. **It doubled the bill.** Every cross-reference needed an [A] and a [B] version, and a contingency
   section switched between them. No parent or legislator could read it.
2. **S.B. 174's future is uncertain.**
   - It has been in House Judiciary since 19 November 2025.
   - The committee accepted a substitute (l_136_0493-6) on 13 May 2026 and held hearings through
     10 June 2026, when a "possible vote" was listed.
   - As of 4 October 2026 it has not been reported, and no further meeting is scheduled.
   - It dies on 31 December 2026 unless enacted.
   - A bill drafted against a 150-section rewrite that may never exist is a bill drafted against
     nothing.
3. **Current law is a better platform than it first appeared.** Ohio already separates legal
   status from schedule (R.C. 3109.04(L)(6)) and already has a motion to convert sole custody to
   shared parenting (R.C. 3109.04(E)(1)(b)). The bill needed to *adjust* those tools, not import
   S.B. 174's.
4. **It is cleaner politically.** A short bill that amends familiar sections is easy to explain,
   and easy to attach as an amendment to S.B. 174 or its successor if that is the vehicle. An
   overlay on S.B. 174 can only ever be an amendment to S.B. 174.

**What was borrowed from S.B. 174:**

- its no-inference-from-temporary-orders rule (proposed R.C. 3109.0411), placed in R.C. 3109.043(C);
- its 28-day hearing clocks, which originate in Civ.R. 75(N) anyway;
- its preponderance standard for restrictions;
- the House substitute's "least restrictive" principle and its mandatory compensatory time.

**If S.B. 174 is enacted,** a short conforming amendment re-points each section to its S.B. 174
successor. The substance does not change. Section 6 states that intent. It does not try to
self-execute, which is what made Revision 1 so long.

## Part 4. Core rule: equal parental status (R.C. 3109.03, 3109.042)

**Problem.** R.C. 3109.042(A) makes an unmarried mother "the sole residential parent and legal
custodian" until a court orders otherwise. A father whose paternity is final still has to sue,
under R.C. 3109.12, to be "grant[ed]" parenting time. No prior Ohio bill fixed this. H.B. 508,
H.B. 14, and S.B. 174 all carried the rule forward.

**Solution.**
- R.C. 3109.03's existing equality sentence is extended from "husband and wife" to "the parents of
  a child," whether or not they were ever married.
- R.C. 3109.042(A) is rewritten in place: once the second parent's parentage is final, both are
  residential parents and legal custodians, with no court action needed.

**Status is not a schedule.** New 3109.042(B) says so in one sentence.

**What about the time before any order?** Unmarried parents are now in exactly the position married
parents are in before divorce: both are custodians, neither has a court schedule, and either may
seek a temporary order (with a 28-day hearing on request) under R.C. 3109.043.

**Considered and rejected:** Revision 1's interim statutory schedule applying by operation of law.
It contradicted the status/schedule principle, and it would have created a schedule nobody asked a
court for.

**Existing orders.** 3109.042(C) keeps any existing order in force. The parent moves for equal
status under the new 3109.04(E)(1)(c). Nothing is rewritten automatically.

## Part 5. The 50/50 starting point (R.C. 3109.044)

**One presumption,** rebuttable by a preponderance: "shared parenting with substantially equal
parenting time." It applies to every first allocation, including temporary orders.

**The model is the enacted states.** They have converged on a short presumption, rebuttable by a
preponderance:

| State | Presumption | Enacted | Rebuttal standard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kentucky | KRS 403.270(2) | 2018 | Preponderance |
| Arkansas | Ark. Code § 9-13-101 | 2021 | Clear and convincing (the outlier) |
| West Virginia | W.Va. Code § 48-9-102a | 2022 | Preponderance |
| Florida | Fla. Stat. § 61.13(2)(c) | 2023 | Preponderance |
| Missouri | RSMo 452.375, S.B. 35 (2023) | 2023 | Preponderance |

Missouri is the closest neighbor model. It was not in Revision 1, and its exceptions are the same
two Revision 2 uses: parental agreement and domestic violence.

**What keeps it short:**
- **No second list of rebuttal grounds.** Ohio's R.C. 3109.04(F)(1) factors and 3109.04(C) are the
  rebuttal evidence.
- **One "not enough" sentence** (conflict, objection, self-created non-cooperation, an unfound
  allegation).
- **No reverse presumption.** Where there is a protection order or a 3109.04(C) conviction or
  adjudication, the presumption simply does not apply, and ordinary best interest governs. Ohio
  already tells the court to weigh those facts against shared parenting (3109.04(C)).
- **No opt-in.** It overrides the current rule that shared parenting requires a party's plan
  (3109.04(A)(1), (D)(1), (G)). If no plan is filed, the court issues one.

**"Substantially equal" is defined** as at least 45 percent of overnights or the equivalent. That
makes it a number, not an adjective.

**The floor: kept, but reduced to one sentence.** The instruction was to reevaluate the Utah-style
hour-by-hour schedule.

- It is cut. Its content (alternate weekends plus a weekday evening) would have written "every other
  weekend" into the Revised Code as the statutory norm.
- It is replaced by: no parent gets fewer than **90 overnights a year** (or the equivalent) unless
  the parents agree, that parent asks for less, distance makes it impracticable, or the court
  finds that more time would endanger the child.
- **Why 90:** every Ohio domestic-relations lawyer already knows the number from R.C. 3119.051, the
  child-support adjustment threshold. That avoids inventing a new unit. Minnesota's enacted floor
  (25 percent, Minn. Stat. § 518.175 subd. 1(g)) is the same size.
- **Why keep any floor at all:** without one, "rebut 50/50, then order every other Saturday" needs
  no safety finding.
- **The long-absent parent:** the endangerment exception covers phased reintroduction, so the
  separate graduated-schedule rule is gone.
- The statute says in bold that 90 is a floor, not a target.

## Part 6. Existing orders: equal status by motion (R.C. 3109.04(E)(1)(c))

**This reverses Revision 1**, which grandfathered existing orders indefinitely.

**The cleanest Ohio mechanism already exists.**
- R.C. 3109.04(E)(1)(b) lets a parent ask to convert a sole-custody decree into shared parenting. It
  is gated by the change-in-circumstances test in (E)(1)(a) and by the shared-parenting-plan
  requirement.
- *Fisher v. Hasenjager*, 116 Ohio St.3d 53, 2007-Ohio-5589, confirms that changing who is a
  residential parent and legal custodian requires a change in circumstances.
- New (E)(1)(c) is a sibling of (E)(1)(b) **without those two gates**:
  - no change in circumstances;
  - no plan;
  - the court "shall grant" unless one of three narrow findings is made: proven history of physical
    violence or abuse; immediate threat of physical harm; or an existing statutory bar
    (rape/sexual battery, R.C. 3109.042(D); killing the other parent, R.C. 3109.42).

**The result is a shared parenting decree.** Under R.C. 3109.04(L)(6), each parent is a residential
parent and legal custodian "regardless of where the child is physically located." That is the
status/schedule distinction, already in Ohio law. The bill does not need to invent it.

**The schedule does not move.** The order says, in bold, that:
- the parenting time schedule, child support, school placement, and every other term carry over
  as terms of the new decree;
- equal status "does not entitle either parent to have the child at any time not provided for in
  the existing schedule."

So the father in the instruction's example (sole custody to mother, zero time to father) becomes an
equal legal custodian with zero scheduled time until a court changes the schedule. He has no
self-help right.

**How he then asks for time.**
- *Braatz v. Braatz*, 85 Ohio St.3d 40 (1999), holds that a parenting-time modification needs **no
  change in circumstances**, only best interest under R.C. 3109.051(D). Revision 2 lets the two
  motions be filed together.
- Revision 2 does **not** apply the 50/50 presumption to that modification motion. The parent comes
  in as an equal (R.C. 3109.03), with no residential-parent "retention" thumb on the scale. The
  presumption is reserved for first allocations.
- This is the floodgate answer: the bill opens no schedule litigation that *Braatz* does not already
  allow.
- If the sponsor wants the presumption to apply to post-restoration schedule motions, that is
  Option 1 in Part 10, with its cost stated.

**What does change.**
- Decision-making becomes joint.
- Deadlock is handled by § 3109.044(G): the existing arrangement continues until a court decides.
  For example, the child stays in the current school.
- Records access follows status.

**Due process and retroactivity** are analyzed in Document 7, Part 1. In short:
- custody decrees are always modifiable, so they create no vested right;
- the new rule operates prospectively, from the date of the new order;
- the other parent gets notice and a hearing on the safety grounds.

## Part 7. Only a court changes a court order (R.C. 3109.045)

**Problem.** Parents report that time ordered by a court is cut, in practice, by someone who is not
a judge:
- a GAL "suspends" visits pending a report;
- a counselor decides when reunification may proceed;
- a caseworker's safety plan says a father may not see his child;
- a police officer at an exchange calls it "a civil matter" and lets the child stay.

**Ohio law points the same way, but only in scattered places.**
- Courts have said "[t]he trial court may not delegate judicial authority" to a GAL (*Weisgarber*,
  2009-Ohio-20).
- Due process requires independent judicial review of a parenting coordinator's decisions
  (*Gregory*, 2019-Ohio-5210).
- An order leaving parenting time to a therapist's discretion was reversed (*In re K.R.H.*,
  2026-Ohio-583).
- The Rules of Superintendence say GAL reports "shall not be considered determinative" (Sup.R.
  8.06(A)(3)). They forbid using parenting coordination for "substantive changes in parenting time"
  (Sup.R. 6.42(B)).

But there is no per se rule. In 2026 one district affirmed a delegation because the objection was not
preserved (*Weinsziehr*, 2026-Ohio-2760). None of it is in a statute that parents, police, or
caseworkers would ever see (Document 1, § 4).

**Solution.** One section, five divisions:
- (A) the order stands as written until a court changes it, and the list of people who cannot change
  it is named; parents may still agree to swap a day;
- (B) a GAL investigates, reports, and recommends, and a recommendation is evidence with no effect
  until the court adopts it; a party may cross-examine the GAL (extending *In re Hoffman*,
  2002-Ohio-5368, beyond permanent custody); any delegation of power to change parenting time to a
  non-judge is unenforceable;
- (C) a parenting coordinator keeps its Sup.R. 6.40–6.46 role of resolving implementation disputes,
  but cannot reduce or suspend time, impose supervision, or change custody; its decisions get
  independent court review on objection (*Gregory*);
- (D) a children-services safety plan binds a parent's court-ordered time only with that parent's
  written agreement or a court order; an agency that sees danger has emergency remedies under
  Chapter 2151;
- (E) protection orders, emergency custody under R.C. 2151.31, ex parte orders, and action to prevent
  imminent harm are untouched.

**Who it targets.** Unauthorized quasi-judicial power, not support services. Nothing limits
investigation, reporting, mediation, or coordination.

## Part 8. Enforcement and the criminal provision

**Civil enforcement is in the existing contempt statute, made faster and more certain:**
- **R.C. 3109.051(K)(1):** "may" becomes "shall" for compensatory time of the same type and duration,
  taken within a year. This follows Texas (2025), Michigan's "same type and duration … within 1 year"
  (MCL 552.642), and the S.B. 174 House substitute.
- **(K)(2):** hearing within 28 days. Prima facie case: the order, the time that should have occurred,
  and evidence it did not. After that, the burden of producing a lawful excuse shifts; the burden of
  persuasion does not. Civil contempt keeps its existing clear-and-convincing standard, so no new
  burden is invented.
- **(K)(3):** one list of lawful excuses, used by both the civil and the criminal rules. It covers
  emergency, illness, weather, transportation, circumstances outside a person's control, agreed
  changes, compliance with other orders, and good-faith protective action that is promptly
  reported.
- **(K)(4):** child refusal. Reasonable efforts are defined as conduct; physical force is never
  required; refusal alone is no excuse for a parent who did not try.
- **(K)(5):** a third contempt finding is a change in circumstances (Texas Fam. Code § 156.107).
- **The three-rung ladder is gone.** R.C. 2705.05 already escalates contempt penalties: up to $250
  and 30 days, then $500 and 60 days, then $1,000 and 90 days.

**Criminal: amend R.C. 2919.23 rather than enact 2919.232.** Three options were weighed.

- **(A) Amend 2919.23.** *Parra* (6th Dist. 2013), *Sprinkle* (12th Dist. 2007), and *Brickles*
  (2d Dist. 1999) already hold that a custodial parent who withholds court-ordered time "keeps" the
  child "without privilege."
  - The offense exists. What is missing is a version a police officer can recognize, a version that
    reaches only serious repeated conduct, and a statement that it is not "a civil matter."
  - Adding one division to the existing section does that with the least new text.
- **(B) Keep new 2919.232.** This produces two overlapping offenses for the same conduct, election
  and allied-offense rules to sort them, and twelve divisions. Rejected.
- **(C) Do nothing criminal, and rely on *Parra*.** This leaves a single reckless missed visit
  technically chargeable as a first-degree misdemeanor (*Parra*'s reading) in some districts and
  undecided in others. That is the worst of both worlds: too broad on paper, unused in practice.
  Rejected.

**The one place Revision 2 narrows existing law.** New 2919.23(E)(3) channels plain withholding of
parenting time into the new division. It requires:
- **knowing** conduct, not reckless;
- **repeated** conduct: three periods in 90 days, 14 consecutive days, or any withholding after a
  contempt finding within the past two years.

A single knowing missed weekend is then a contempt matter, not a crime, unless the child is
concealed or taken out of Ohio, which stay under (A)(1).

This is deliberate. It is what "serious, knowing, repeated" means, and it makes the statute
defensible. The sponsor should know it is a narrowing. Option 3 in Part 10 is to leave (A)(1)
untouched.

**Not a civil matter.** 2919.23(E)(4) codifies *Parra* ¶¶ 17–18: civil remedies do not displace
investigation or prosecution.

**Referral and declination notice (R.C. 3109.051(K)(6), 2919.23(G)).**
- **Who refers.** The referring body is the **court**. It is the one body that will already have made
  findings, under oath and with both sides heard, so a referral arrives with a record. That is the
  simplest design: no new agency and no fiscal note.
- **Agency option.** If an agency is wanted, Michigan's Friend of the Court (MCL 552.511b, 552.641 to
  552.644) is the tested model, and Ohio's county child support enforcement agencies are the natural
  host. That is Option 4 in Part 10.
  - *The planning discussion this mechanism grew out of is not in the project files. If it chose a
    different designated body, substitute it in (K)(6). Nothing else changes.*
- **The prosecutor's duty.** Within 60 days, extendable once by 30, the prosecutor must file a charge
  or send written notice of declining. No reasons are required.
- **Why it respects prosecutorial discretion.**
  - It is a ministerial notice duty. Ohio already imposes duties of this kind on prosecutors, e.g.
    R.C. 2930.05(B)(2) (inform the victim on electing not to seek a bond change) and 2930.06 (confer
    before dismissal).
  - It never compels a charge, consistent with *State ex rel. Master v. Cleveland* (1996) and *State
    ex rel. Rankin v. State*, 2025-Ohio-4483. Rankin arose from an interference-with-custody case.
  - A declination is expressly unreviewable and is no bar to later prosecution.
- **Parents' independent access is preserved.** They can still report to police, or file a private
  affidavit under R.C. 2935.09.

## Part 9. Allegations and the 36-month outside limit (R.C. 3109.046)

**Problem.** A restriction imposed "pending investigation" can last years. The agency closes its
file as "unsubstantiated" or "indicated," no one is charged, and the court never decides whether
the allegation is true. Meanwhile the separation becomes the status quo the court then protects.

**When is an allegation "resolved"?** The simplest defensible test uses existing Ohio standards
only: a **court finding, after a hearing at which the restricted parent could present evidence**,
on whether the allegation is true. That covers:
- the custody court's own finding (preponderance);
- a juvenile adjudication (clear and convincing, R.C. 2151.35);
- a protection-order full hearing;
- a criminal conviction.

It deliberately excludes:
- **agency dispositions.** "Substantiated" can rest on "other forms of confirmation deemed valid by
  the PCSA" (Ohio Adm.Code 5180:2-1-01). That is an administrative label, not an adjudication.
- **charging decisions**, dismissals, and acquittals. An acquittal means "not proven beyond a
  reasonable doubt," not "false."

This keeps the bill from collapsing allegation, investigation, substantiation, charging,
adjudication, and falsity into one another.

**The mechanism has three layers, plus a safeguard:**
1. **A 90-day hearing on request** (§ 3109.046(C)). This mirrors Ohio's own juvenile rule that a
   dispositional hearing in an abuse case "shall not be held more than ninety days after" the
   complaint (R.C. 2151.35(B)(1)). A pending investigation or the lack of a charge does not stop the
   court from deciding.
2. **A 36-month outside limit**, as instructed. At 30 months the court must set the allegation for
   hearing on its own motion, so the deadline is a calendar entry rather than a cliff. After 36
   months, the restriction ends and the prior order resumes. If there was none, the court issues a
   § 3109.044 order within 28 days.
3. **One continuance, capped at 90 days**, on written findings. No serial extensions. The same
   allegation cannot be recycled; new conduct can support a new restriction.

**Why 90 days for the one continuance.** These are the Ohio analogs:

| Analog | Citation | What it allows |
|---|---|---|
| Abuse-case disposition | R.C. 2151.35(B)(1) | One extension of at most 45 days past a 90-day deadline, then mandatory dismissal without prejudice. This is the closest analog: one extension, for fact-finding, capped. |
| Adjudicatory hearing | R.C. 2151.28(A)(2) | Continuance for "any necessary evaluation," but no later than 60 days after filing. |
| Temporary custody | R.C. 2151.415(D) | Two six-month extensions. These measure reunification *progress*, not fact-finding, so they are the wrong analog. |

Ninety days is two of the 2151.35 increments. That leaves room to schedule a contested evidentiary
hearing with expert witnesses on a common pleas docket, but not enough to reopen investigation. If
the sponsor wants the tightest tested number, use 45 (Option 5).

**False allegations.**
- Good-faith reports are protected. Unsubstantiated, unprosecuted, unproven, or mistaken does not
  mean false.
- A **knowingly false** allegation requires three things, on the ordinary civil standard:
  1. material falsity;
  2. knowledge;
  3. purpose to interfere.
- The consequences remove the benefit of the lie:
  - make-up time for the time lost;
  - fees;
  - a change in circumstances.
- R.C. 2921.14 already makes a knowingly false child-abuse report a first-degree misdemeanor, so no
  new crime is needed. Note: by its terms it reaches only permissive reports under 2151.421(B).
- The Kentucky anti-weaponization rule is kept: a protective parent's good-faith action is not
  "failure to facilitate."

**Scope.** Protection orders and Chapter 2151 cases have their own timelines (7 to 10 days; 90 days)
and are excluded. A custody restriction that *relies on* one of them is covered.

## Part 10. Sponsor options (genuine choices, not oversights)

1. **Apply the 50/50 presumption to schedule motions after restoration of status.**
   - *Gain:* fully achieves the H.B. 14 goal for existing families.
   - *Cost:* this is precisely the "floodgate" the Ohio State Bar Association used against H.B. 14,
     and its strongest objection.
   - *Middle path:* apply it only to motions filed within 24 months of the act.
2. **Rebuttal standard for a knowingly false allegation:** preponderance (as drafted) or clear and
   convincing. The triple element (falsity, knowledge, purpose) is the real protection.
   - Clear and convincing would draw domestic-violence advocates toward neutrality, at modest cost.
3. **Leave 2919.23(A)(1) untouched.** Drop (E)(3) and keep *Parra*'s broader single-incident reach.
   - *Cost:* the narrow, defensible offense then overlaps a broad one, and the "criminalizing custody
     disputes" objection returns.
4. **Designated enforcement body.**
   - *Default:* the court.
   - *Alternative:* designate county child support enforcement agencies to receive parenting-time
     complaints, apply make-up time, start contempt, and make referrals, on the Michigan Friend of
     the Court model. This needs a fiscal note and county buy-in.
5. **Continuance length** under § 3109.046(D): 90 days (as drafted) or 45 days (R.C. 2151.35(B)(1)).
6. **Data report** (Section 5): keep it as drafted (cheap; requested by National Parents
   Organization; contained in H.B. 508), or cut it for length.

## Part 11. Political posture

The opponents of H.B. 14 and H.B. 508 made these objections, and Revision 2 answers each in the
text:

| Objection | Who made it | Answer in the text |
|---|---|---|
| Clear and convincing raises costs | Ohio State Bar Association | Preponderance throughout |
| "Detrimental" is the wrong test | Ohio State Bar Association | Best interest throughout |
| One size fits all | Ohio State Bar Association | Agreement, distance, and safety exceptions; a court-built maximize-time schedule |
| Endangers domestic-violence victims | Ohio Domestic Violence Network | Presumption off for protection-order respondents and 3109.04(C) offenders; no conviction needed to protect; good-faith reports protected; anti-weaponization rule |
| Floodgate | Ohio State Bar Association | Status by motion, schedule untouched; *Braatz* already allows best-interest schedule motions |
| Undermines judges | Ohio State Bar Association | The bill takes power away from non-judges and gives it back to judges |

The last row is the new argument. Revision 2 gives judges *more* exclusive authority, not less.
