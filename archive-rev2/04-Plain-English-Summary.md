# DOCUMENT 4 — PLAIN-ENGLISH SUMMARY

## The Ohio Equal Parenting and Parent-Child Relationship Protection Act, in five minutes

*This describes a proposed bill. It is not law.*

---

### 1. Every legal parent is an equal parent.

Today, if you and your child's other parent were never married, Ohio law makes the mother the
sole legal custodian until a father goes to court and wins an order. A married father starts
out equal. An unmarried father does not.

Under this bill, once parentage is legally established (by signing the acknowledgment of
paternity, or by a court or agency finding), **both parents are equal legal parents**. You do
not have to sue to become one.

**Equal status is not a schedule.** Being an equal parent does not by itself say where the child
sleeps on Tuesday. If there is no court order, either parent can ask a court for one, and if a
temporary order is made without a hearing, you can get a hearing within 28 days.

### 2. The starting point is 50/50.

When a court first decides custody, it starts from **shared parenting with substantially equal
time** (at least 45 percent each). Either parent can show that something different is better
for the child, using the ordinary civil standard ("more likely than not"). A conviction is not
required to protect a child.

The starting point **does not apply** to a parent under a protection order, or to a parent
convicted of abusing a family member or found responsible for it.

The following are **not enough by themselves** to defeat 50/50: the parents don't get along;
one parent objects; one parent refuses to cooperate; or someone made an accusation the court
has not found true.

If the judge orders less than equal time, the judge must **say why in writing** and must give
each parent as much time as the child's welfare allows. No parent gets less than **90 overnights
a year** (about one in four) unless the parents agree, that parent asks for less, distance makes
it impossible, or the judge finds more time would endanger the child. **Ninety nights is a
floor, not a goal.**

Temporary orders can't quietly become permanent. A judge may not rely on how long a temporary
order has been in place, or how the child got used to it, as a reason to keep it.

### 3. If you already have a custody order, you can ask to be an equal parent again.

If an existing order makes the other parent the sole custodian, you can file a simple motion
asking the court to make you **both** residential parents and legal custodians. You do **not**
have to prove that circumstances have changed. The court must grant it unless you have a proven
history of physical violence or abuse, or you are an immediate threat of physical harm.

**This restores your status, not a schedule.** Your current parenting-time schedule, child
support, and the child's school all stay exactly as they are until a court changes them. If your
order gives you every other weekend, you still have every other weekend. If it gives you no time,
you still have no time. What changes is that you now come to court as an equal parent when you
ask for more. You can file both requests at once.

### 4. Only a judge can change a court order.

If the order says Tuesday 4 to 7, then Tuesday 4 to 7 it is, until a judge changes it. No one
else can change it, suspend it, or add conditions to it. That includes the other parent, a
guardian ad litem (GAL), a parenting coordinator, a counselor, a children services caseworker, or
a police officer. Parents can still agree to swap a day.

**GALs still matter.** They investigate, report, and recommend. But a GAL's recommendation is
advice to the judge. It changes nothing until the judge signs an order. A children services
"safety plan" can't cut your court-ordered time unless you agree in writing or a judge orders it.
Real emergencies are still handled through protection orders and emergency custody.

### 5. Accusations must be decided, not left hanging.

An accusation by itself doesn't change anything. A judge has to order a restriction, based on
evidence.

If your time is restricted because of an accusation, you can ask for a hearing, and the court must
hold it within 90 days. An open investigation, or the fact that no criminal charge has been filed,
doesn't stop the judge from deciding.

**Outside limit:** a restriction based on an accusation that has never been decided ends after
**36 months**. If the accusation still hasn't been decided at 30 months, the court must set
a hearing on its own. The judge can extend the 36 months once, by up to 90 days, and only by explaining in writing
why the extra time is truly needed. When it ends, your previous schedule comes back. The same
accusation can't be recycled to restrict you again unless a court decides it is true.

**Good-faith reports are protected.** A parent who reports abuse honestly is never punished for
it, even if the report turns out to be mistaken or can't be proven. Only a **knowingly false**
accusation, made to cut the other parent out, has consequences: make-up time for the time lost,
paying the other parent's legal fees, and grounds to change custody.

### 6. Missed parenting time gets made up.

If a court finds the other parent in contempt for denying your parenting time, the judge **must**
order make-up time of the same kind and length, to be used within a year, and must make that
parent pay your court costs and attorney's fees.

To prove it, you show three things: the order, the time you should have had, and evidence that
it didn't happen. The court must hear the case within 28 days.

Missed time is **excused** for real emergencies, illness, weather, car trouble, changes both
parents agreed to, following a protection order, or a good-faith action to protect the child that
is promptly reported.

**If the child refuses:** you must make reasonable efforts, meaning you have the child ready on
time, tell the child about the visit, don't discourage it, and let the other parent know if the
child refuses. You never have to use physical force. A child's refusal alone doesn't excuse a
parent who didn't try. The judge can still listen to an older child's wishes.

A **third** contempt finding is grounds to change custody.

### 7. Serious, repeated withholding is a crime.

It is a first-degree misdemeanor to **knowingly** keep a child from a parent during that parent's
court-ordered time:
- three or more times in 90 days;
- for 14 days or more in a row; or
- after already being held in contempt for it within the last two years.

It becomes a felony if it happens again after a conviction, or if the child is hidden or taken out
of Ohio. The same excuses listed above apply, and so does a reasonable reading of an unclear
order. **It is not "just a civil matter."** Police can investigate and prosecutors can prosecute,
whether or not a contempt case also exists.

A judge who finds this kind of conduct can refer it to the prosecutor. The prosecutor decides
whether to charge. But within 60 days (90 with one extension), the prosecutor must either file
charges or send written notice of declining. No reasons are required.

### 8. What this bill does not do.

- It does not change how child support is calculated.
- It does not automatically rewrite anyone's parenting schedule.
- It does not punish anyone for an honest report of abuse.
- It does not use the term "parental alienation" or any psychological theory. It deals only with
  things a person actually did.
- It does not take away a judge's power to protect a child.
